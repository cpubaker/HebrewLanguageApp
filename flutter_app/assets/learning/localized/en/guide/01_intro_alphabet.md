# Getting to know Hebrew

Hebrew is the modern language of Israel with ancient roots. For many centuries it was rarely spoken in daily life: it survived chiefly as a language of prayer, study, and writing. At the end of the nineteenth century, Hebrew began to revive as a living spoken language, and its connection with ancient tradition is still easy to feel today.

Before going deeper into grammar, it helps to know a few basic facts about how Hebrew works in writing:

- Hebrew is written from right to left;
- consonants form the core of a word;
- vowels are often shown by separate signs above, below, or inside letters;
- the alphabet is called the aleph-bet and has 22 main letters;
- five of those letters have a special form at the end of a word.

A few simple words give you an immediate sense of what Hebrew looks like on the page:

- אִמָּא – ima – mother
- בַּיִת – bayit – house
- שָׁלוֹם – shalom – peace, hello
- יָד – yad – hand

These words already show some of the letters you will see most often:

- א – aleph
- ב – bet
- מ – mem
- ש – shin
- ל – lamed
- י – yod

You will find the full list of all 22 letters in the separate alphabet lesson.

## Final letter forms

Five letters change their shape when they appear at the very end of a word:

- כ → ך
- מ → ם
- נ → ן
- פ → ף
- צ → ץ

They are not new letters; they are the same letters written differently at the end. It is easiest to see them in whole words rather than in a chart:

- מַיִם – mayim – water: מ at the start of the word and ם at the end
- נָתַן – natan – gave: נ at the start of the word and ן at the end
- כָּךְ – kakh – so, thus: כ at the start of the word and ך at the end

## No capital letters

Hebrew has no distinction between uppercase and lowercase letters, unlike English and many other languages. The same letter looks the same at the beginning of a sentence, in a proper name, and inside a word. No letter therefore signals the beginning of a sentence or a proper name; use context and punctuation instead.
