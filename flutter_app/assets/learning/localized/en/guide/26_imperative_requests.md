# Imperatives and requests

The imperative in Hebrew is used for commands, short instructions, requests, and prohibitions. In real speech, it is important to distinguish:

- the short classical imperative form;
- a softer form that resembles the future tense;
- a polite request;
- a negative command with אל;
- an invitation meaning “let’s.”

At the start, there is no need to master the entire system. It is more useful to recognize a few very common forms and feel the difference between a sharp command, a gentler request, and a prohibition.

## The classical imperative

Hebrew has separate imperative forms for the second person: to a man, to a woman, and to several people. These forms occur more often in short instructions, forms of address, teaching commands, signs, and recipes.

- לֵךְ! – lekh! – go! (to a man)
- לְכִי! – lekhi! – go! (to a woman)
- לְכוּ! – lekhu! – go! (to several people)

- שֵׁב! – shev! – sit down! (to a man)
- שְׁבִי! – shvi! – sit down! (to a woman)
- שְׁבוּ! – shvu! – sit down! (to several people)

- תֵּן! – ten! – give! (to a man)
- תְּנִי! – tni! – give! (to a woman)
- תְּנוּ! – tnu! – give! (to several people)

## A few more common forms

- קְרָא! – kra! – read! (to a man)
- קִרְאִי! – kir'i! – read! (to a woman)
- קִרְאוּ! – kir'u! – read! (to several people)

- כְּתֹב! – ktov! – write! (to a man)
- כִּתְבִי! – kitvi! – write! (to a woman)
- כִּתְבוּ! – kitvu! – write! (to several people)

- שְׁמַע! – shma! – listen! (to a man)
- שִׁמְעִי! – shim'i! – listen! (to a woman)
- שִׁמְעוּ! – shim'u! – listen! (to several people)

- בּוֹא! – bo! – come! (to a man)
- בּוֹאִי! – bo'i! – come! (to a woman)
- בּוֹאוּ! – bo'u! – come! (to several people)

## A softer everyday form

In modern Hebrew, forms that resemble the second-person future tense are often used instead of the “classical” imperative. In ordinary speech, these forms sound more natural and softer. The classical imperative remains common in recipes, instructions, signs, school tasks, and short sharp commands, while the future-like form is more common in conversation.

- תֵּלֵךְ רֶגַע – telekh rega – go for a moment
- תִּקְרָא כָּאן – tikra kan – read here
- תִּשְׁמַע – tishma – listen
- תִּכְתְּבִי לִי – tikhtevi li – write to me (to a woman)
- תָּבוֹא מָחָר – tavo makhar – come tomorrow

## Polite requests

When tone matters as well as content, requests are often softened with an additional word:

- בְּבַקָּשָׁה – bevakasha – please

Examples:

- שֵׁב בְּבַקָּשָׁה – shev bevakasha – sit down, please
- שְׁבִי בְּבַקָּשָׁה – shvi bevakasha – sit down, please (to a woman)
- בּוֹא בְּבַקָּשָׁה – bo bevakasha – come, please
- תַּקְשִׁיב בְּבַקָּשָׁה – takshiv bevakasha – listen, please
- תִּכְתְּבוּ בְּבַקָּשָׁה – tikhtevu bevakasha – write, please

Even softer forms (“could you?”, “I would be glad if...”) are covered separately in the lesson on soft requests.

## Useful classroom commands

For a learning context, these forms are especially common:

- קִרְאוּ אֶת הַטֶּקְסְט – kir'u et ha-tekst – read the text
- כִּתְבוּ אֶת הַמִּלָּה – kitvu et ha-mila – write the word
- שִׁמְעוּ אֶת הַמִּשְׁפָּט – shim'u et ha-mishpat – listen to the sentence
- חִזְרוּ בְּבַקָּשָׁה – khizru bevakasha – repeat, please
- עֲנוּ עַל הַשְּׁאֵלָה – anu al ha-she'ela – answer the question

## Prohibition: אל

For a negative command or prohibition, Hebrew uses אַל with the second-person future-tense form.

- אַל תֵּלֵךְ – al telekh – do not go (to a man)
- אַל תֵּלְכִי – al telkhi – do not go (to a woman)
- אַל תִּכְתְּבוּ – al tikhtevu – do not write
- אַל תִּשְׁכַּח – al tishkakh – do not forget (to a man)
- אַל תִּשְׁכְּחִי – al tishkekhi – do not forget (to a woman)

## “Let’s”: first-person plural as an invitation

To suggest a shared action (“let’s go,” “let’s do it,” “let’s eat”), Hebrew uses the first-person plural future form, either by itself or preceded by בּוֹאוּ.

- נֵלֵךְ! – nelekh! – let’s go!
- נֵלֵךְ לַיָּם – nelekh la-yam – let’s go to the sea
- בּוֹאוּ נֹאכַל – bo'u nokhal – let’s eat
- בּוֹאוּ נְדַבֵּר – bo'u nedaber – let’s talk
- נָנוּחַ קְצָת – nanu'akh ktsat – let’s rest a little

Putting בּוֹאוּ before the form adds an inviting “come on, let’s...” feeling. Without it, the invitation is shorter and more direct.

## Compare

- לֵךְ! – a short imperative form
- תֵּלֵךְ רֶגַע – a softer everyday form
- אַל תֵּלֵךְ – a prohibition
- נֵלֵךְ! – an invitation to a shared action

## Common mistakes

- Expecting the language always to use only the “classical” imperative. In real speech, forms such as תֵּלֵךְ, תִּקְרָא, and תִּכְתֹּב are very common and often sound more natural than lekh, kra, or ktov.
- Ignoring the addressee’s gender. לֵךְ / בּוֹא are used to a man; לְכִי / בּוֹאִי are used to a woman. The plural form is the same for everyone.
- Confusing negation and prohibition. לֹא negates a fact (“I am not going”), while אַל prohibits an action (“don’t go”): compare הוּא לֹא הוֹלֵךְ – he is not going, and אַל תֵּלֵךְ – don’t go.
- Not noticing the “let’s” form. In modern Hebrew, an invitation to a shared action often sounds exactly like the first-person plural future tense (נֵלֵךְ, נֹאכַל, נְדַבֵּר), sometimes with בּוֹאוּ before it.
