# Reading Hebrew

Reading Hebrew can feel unfamiliar at first, not because the letters themselves are unusual, but because a written word gives less detail than an English one. In English, vowels are almost always visible. In Hebrew, consonants form the core of a word, while vowels are either indicated by small signs or left unwritten altogether.

## Nikkud: vowel signs

These small signs are called nikkud. They are diacritics above, below, or inside a letter that directly show how to read a word. You will see nikkud in textbooks, dictionaries, children’s books, and learning materials. Ordinary modern texts—newspapers, books, notices, and messages—usually do not include it. Readers recognize words from their shape, familiar patterns, and context.

Compare the same word with and without nikkud:

- שָׁלוֹם with nikkud is immediately read as shalom
- שלום without nikkud looks barer, but it is the same word

Once you know a word, that is not a problem. When you do not, you need to do more than sound out one letter after another: you have to work out which word you are looking at.

At the beginning, it is enough to know the most common signs. The marks are small; in each example, the sign appears directly in the word:

- ַ or ָ usually give the sound a:
  - יַד – yad – hand
  - אָב – av – father
- ֶ or ֵ usually give the sound e:
  - יֶלֶד – yeled – boy
  - בֵּן – ben – son
- ִ gives the sound i:
  - עִיר – ir – city
  - שִׁיר – shir – song
- ֹ or וֹ give the sound o:
  - לֹא – lo – no
  - תּוֹדָה – toda – thank you
- ֻ or וּ give the sound u:
  - שֻׁלְחָן – shulkhan – table
  - שׁוּק – shuk – market
- ְ is called sheva: it either gives a very short vowel sound or marks the absence of a vowel:
  - שְׁמַע – shma – listen

## Dots in the letters themselves

Apart from nikkud, a few dots affect the consonant rather than the vowel. A small dot inside a letter is called a dagesh and changes its sound:

- בּ – b, while ב – v
- כּ – k, while כ – kh
- פּ – p, while פ – f

For the letter ש, the dot is above the letter and tells you which sound it has:

- שׁ (dot on the right) – sh
- שׂ (dot on the left) – s

## Reading without nikkud

When nikkud is present, it is best to remember a word as a whole: how it looks, how it sounds, and where the stress falls. This is faster than rebuilding it letter by letter every time.

Without nikkud, the letters ו and י suggest some vowels, and many words simply become familiar through experience. For an unfamiliar word without nikkud, try this strategy:

- first identify the letters themselves;
- check whether there is a ו or י inside the word: they often point to part of a vowel;
- think about what fits the meaning: a noun, a verb, or an adjective;
- recall whether you have seen the word with nikkud or heard it in audio;
- as a last resort, read it approximately and check a dictionary or audio.

## When spelling is ambiguous

Sometimes you really cannot know the correct pronunciation without context. That is not your mistake; it is an ordinary feature of Hebrew writing. The same spelling can stand for different words.

- ספר can be read as sefer – book, or safar – he counted
- מלך can be read as melekh – king, or malakh – he reigned
- מורה can be read as more – teacher, or mora – female teacher

That is why Hebrew is read as words in a sentence, not as isolated words in a vacuum. Context usually resolves the ambiguity at once: if the sentence already contains another verb, your word is probably a noun; if an action is needed, the same spelling is more likely to be a verb.

## How the skill develops

In practice, reading develops in two stages. First, you read words with nikkud and get used to the way Hebrew marks vowels. Then you gradually begin to recognize the same words without it. This is a normal path. Reading modern Hebrew well means more than knowing the letters: it means quickly recognizing familiar words and calmly accepting uncertainty where context cannot yet settle it.
