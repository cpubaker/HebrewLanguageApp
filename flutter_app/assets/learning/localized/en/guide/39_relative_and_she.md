# ש־: that, who, which

After basic subordinate constructions, learners quickly encounter another extremely common pattern: ש־. It helps say “the book that I am reading,” “the person who lives here,” and “I know that he is at home.” In modern Hebrew, it is one of the most useful connectors for more complex sentences.

The main difficulty is usually not the form itself, but the habit of not looking for a separate English equivalent every time. It is much more useful to see how one short connector naturally joins several types of sentences.

ש־ very often has two major roles:

- it connects a noun to an explanatory part: “who / which / that”;
- it introduces a clause after words such as “know,” “think,” and “say”: “that.”

At the start, it is best to treat ש־ not as a set of separate translations, but as one universal living connector.

## When ש־ means “who / which / that”

In these sentences, an object or person comes first, followed by a part that clarifies it.

- הַסֵּפֶר שֶׁאֲנִי קוֹרֵא – ha-sefer she-ani kore – the book that I am reading
- הַיֶּלֶד שֶׁגָּר בָּעִיר – ha-yeled she-gar ba-ir – the boy who lives in the city
- הַחֲבֵרָה שֶׁעוֹבֶדֶת אִתִּי – ha-khavera she-ovedet iti – the friend who works with me
- הַמָּקוֹם שֶׁאֲנִי אוֹהֵב – ha-makom she-ani ohev – the place that I love

Here ש־ does not change for gender and number as English “who / which” may. This is often surprising for Ukrainian learners.

## When ש־ means “that”

After verbs such as “know,” “think,” “say,” “see,” and “remember,” ש־ often introduces a whole thought.

- אֲנִי יוֹדֵעַ שֶׁהוּא בַּבַּיִת – ani yode'a she-hu ba-bayit – I know that he is at home
- הִיא חוֹשֶׁבֶת שֶׁאֲנַחְנוּ נָבוֹא – hi khoshevet she-anakhnu navo – she thinks that we will come
- אֲנַחְנוּ רוֹאִים שֶׁהִיא עֲיֵפָה – anakhnu ro'im she-hi ayefa – we see that she is tired
- הוּא אוֹמֵר שֶׁאֵין זְמַן – hu omer she-ein zman – he says that there is no time

In these patterns, ש־ connects not a noun and its description, but one thought with another.

## The same ש־ in two roles

It is useful to see the difference side by side:

- הַסֵּפֶר שֶׁאֲנִי קוֹרֵא – the book that I am reading (a noun clarification)
- אֲנִי יוֹדֵעַ שֶׁאֲנִי קוֹרֵא לְאַט – I know that I read slowly (a subordinate thought)

In both cases there is ש־, but in the first it clarifies a noun, while in the second it introduces a whole subordinate thought.

## When the clarification requires a preposition

If the English translation has a preposition (“in which,” “about which,” “with whom”), Hebrew does not put it at the beginning of the relative clause. Instead, it comes at the end with a pronominal suffix that refers back to the main noun.

- הַבַּיִת שֶׁאֲנַחְנוּ גָּרִים בּוֹ – ha-bayit she-anakhnu garim bo – the house in which we live (in it)
- הַסֵּפֶר שֶׁדִּבַּרְתִּי עָלָיו – ha-sefer she-dibarti alav – the book that I spoke about (about it)
- הָאִישׁ שֶׁעָבַדְתִּי אִתּוֹ – ha-ish she-avadti ito – the man with whom I worked (with him)
- הָעֵט שֶׁאֲנִי מִשְׁתַּמֵּשׁ בּוֹ – ha-et she-ani mishtamesh bo – the pen that I use (with it)

The preposition (ב, על, עם, מ...) does not disappear. It stands at the end of the subordinate clause, and its suffix refers back to the main noun.

This is one of the patterns where Hebrew and Ukrainian organize thought differently. At first, it is useful to memorize ready-made pairs such as שֶׁ... בּוֹ, שֶׁ... עָלָיו, and שֶׁ... אִתּוֹ.

## How this looks with a definite word

Very often, a ש־ construction follows a definite noun:

- הַסֵּפֶר שֶׁאֲנִי קוֹרֵא
- הַיַּלְדָּה שֶׁאַתְּ מְדַבֶּרֶת אִתָּהּ
- הַבַּיִת שֶׁאֲנַחְנוּ גָּרִים בּוֹ

At the beginning, do not break this pattern into too many tiny pieces; treat it as one block: object + clarification.

## A simple way to build these sentences

Think of it this way:

1. name the object or person;
2. add ש־;
3. say a short sentence explaining who or what it is;
4. if a preposition is required, put it with a suffix at the end of the clause.

Examples:

- הַיֶּלֶד + ש + גָּר פֹּה → הַיֶּלֶד שֶׁגָּר פֹּה – the boy who lives here
- הַסֵּפֶר + ש + אֲנִי רוֹצֶה → הַסֵּפֶר שֶׁאֲנִי רוֹצֶה – the book that I want
- הָאִשָּׁה + ש + עוֹבֶדֶת כָּאן → הָאִשָּׁה שֶׁעוֹבֶדֶת כָּאן – the woman who works here
- הַבַּיִת + ש + אֲנִי גָּר בּוֹ → הַבַּיִת שֶׁאֲנִי גָּר בּוֹ – the house in which I live

## The connector after words of thought and speech

ש־ is especially useful after these very common words:

- יוֹדֵעַ / יוֹדַעַת – know
- חוֹשֵׁב / חוֹשֶׁבֶת – think
- אוֹמֵר / אוֹמֶרֶת – say
- רוֹאֶה / רוֹאִים – see
- זוֹכֵר / זוֹכֶרֶת – remember

Examples:

- אֲנִי חוֹשֵׁב שֶׁהוּא עוֹבֵד – I think that he is working
- הִיא אוֹמֶרֶת שֶׁאַתְּ בָּאָה מָחָר – she says that you will come tomorrow
- אֲנַחְנוּ זוֹכְרִים שֶׁהָיִיתָ פֹּה – we remember that you were here

## Do not make a sentence unnecessarily long

ש־ is useful, but not every sentence needs to be expanded to the limit. If you already have a main idea and one needed clarification, that is usually enough.

Compare:

- אֲנִי מַכִּיר אֶת הָאִישׁ שֶׁגָּר כָּאן – I know the man who lives here
- אֲנִי מַכִּיר אֶת הָאִישׁ שֶׁגָּר כָּאן וְעוֹבֵד עִם דָּנָה וּמְחַפֵּשׂ דִּירָה חֲדָשָׁה – I know the man who lives here, works with Dana, and is looking for a new apartment

The second phrase is possible but harder to process. In real speech, it is often more natural to say:

- אֲנִי מַכִּיר אֶת הָאִישׁ שֶׁגָּר כָּאן. הוּא עוֹבֵד עִם דָּנָה וּמְחַפֵּשׂ דִּירָה חֲדָשָׁה.

This is not simplification for a lower level; it is a normal strategy for clear language.

## Why this is difficult for Ukrainian learners

Ukrainian clearly distinguishes “that” from “who / which.” In modern Hebrew, ש־ often covers both types of connection. At first, one word can feel insufficient, but that is how the language works.

## What to pay attention to

When you see ש־, ask yourself immediately:

- is it clarifying a noun, or introducing a thought after “I know / think / say”?
- if it clarifies a noun, does a preposition with a suffix belong at the end of the subordinate clause (בּוֹ, עָלָיו, אִתּוֹ)?
- where is the main clause and where is the subordinate one?

## Common mistakes

- Expecting separate forms for “who / which.” This is natural in Ukrainian, but modern Hebrew very often uses the one connector ש־.
- Confusing a question with a relative construction. אֵיזֶה סֵפֶר? – which book? (a question). הַסֵּפֶר שֶׁאֲנִי קוֹרֵא – the book that I am reading (a clarification).
- Not seeing the boundary between main and subordinate clauses. In אֲנִי יוֹדֵעַ שֶׁהוּא בַּבַּיִת, see two parts: אֲנִי יוֹדֵעַ + שֶׁהוּא בַּבַּיִת.
- Omitting the preposition with its suffix at the end. Correct: הַבַּיִת שֶׁאֲנִי גָּר בּוֹ. Not: הַבַּיִת שֶׁאֲנִי גָּר. The preposition cannot simply disappear if it belonged in the original phrase.
