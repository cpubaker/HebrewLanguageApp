# Typical mistakes for Ukrainian speakers

Most mistakes arise not from not knowing an individual word, but from carrying a Ukrainian pattern into Hebrew. That is why this lesson is best read not as a list of “errors,” but as a map of common places where Ukrainian intuition can mislead.

This approach helps remove the unnecessary feeling of “I am always making mistakes.” What matters more is seeing repeated risk points and rebuilding a familiar pattern in time.

When building a phrase, it is useful to check not only the meaning of a word, but the whole construction:

- is the article ה־ needed?
- should it be לא or אין?
- has זה been inserted unnecessarily?
- does the form agree in gender?
- has a preposition been translated literally?
- has the whole construction been copied from Ukrainian?

## Definiteness: omitting ה־

Because Ukrainian has no article, it is easy to omit Hebrew ה־ where it is needed: before a specific object, person, or place.

Compare:

- אֲנִי קוֹרֵא סֵפֶר – ani kore sefer – I am reading a book (some book)
- אֲנִי קוֹרֵא אֶת הַסֵּפֶר – ani kore et ha-sefer – I am reading the book
- יֵשׁ לִי עֵט – yesh li et – I have a pen
- הָעֵט עַל הַשֻּׁלְחָן – ha-et al ha-shulkhan – the pen is on the table

If the other person already understands exactly what is being discussed, Hebrew often needs the article. This is where a Ukrainian speaker most often relies on first-language intuition and misses it.

## Negation: לא or אין?

Ukrainian has one familiar word for “not,” but in Hebrew these patterns diverge:

- לא negates an action, state, or quality;
- אין shows that something is absent.

It is important not only to know this distinction, but to check it each time in a ready-made phrase.

Correct patterns:

- אֲנִי לֹא עוֹבֵד הַיּוֹם – ani lo oved ha-yom – I am not working today
- אֲנִי לֹא עֲיֵפָה – ani lo ayefa – I am not tired
- אֵין לִי זְמַן – ein li zman – I do not have time
- אֵין פֹּה מַיִם – ein po mayim – there is no water here

A typical calque is לֹא לִי זְמַן. The correct form is אֵין לִי זְמַן.

## Age: do not copy the Ukrainian model

The Ukrainian model “I have twenty years” does not transfer well to Hebrew. The natural basic construction for age uses בֶּן or בַּת.

Correct patterns:

- אֲנִי בֶּן עֶשְׂרִים – ani ben esrim – I am twenty years old (said by a man)
- אֲנִי בַּת עֶשְׂרִים – ani bat esrim – I am twenty years old (said by a woman)
- הַיֶּלֶד בֶּן חָמֵשׁ – ha-yeled ben khamesh – the boy is five years old
- הַיַּלְדָּה בַּת חָמֵשׁ – ha-yalda bat khamesh – the girl is five years old

A calque from Ukrainian is יֵשׁ לִי עֶשְׂרִים שָׁנִים. In basic speech, this sounds unnatural.

## Nominal sentences: do not add זה unnecessarily

In the present tense, sentences about a profession, role, state, or place are usually built without a separate word equivalent to “this.”

Correct patterns:

- אֲנִי מוֹרָה – ani mora – I am a teacher (said by a woman)
- הוּא תַּלְמִיד – hu talmid – he is a student
- הִיא בַּבַּיִת – hi ba-bayit – she is at home

זה has a different role when you genuinely want to say “this”:

- זֶה אָח שֶׁלִּי – ze akh sheli – this is my brother

So זה works well as “this,” but should not be inserted mechanically into every nominal sentence.

## Gender in address: do not use masculine by default

In Ukrainian, the form of “you study” or “you want” does not change for gender. In Hebrew it does, and this is one of the most common mistakes in real speech.

Compare:

- אַתָּה לוֹמֵד – ata lomed – you study (to a man)
- אַתְּ לוֹמֶדֶת – at lomedet – you study (to a woman)
- אַתָּה רוֹצֶה – ata rotse – you want (to a man)
- אַתְּ רוֹצָה – at rotsa – you want (to a woman)

## Prepositions: do not translate word for word

Prepositions rarely match completely between languages. As a result, literal translation often creates an unnatural or incorrect phrase. This is one of the most typical areas of interference for Ukrainian learners.

Common patterns:

- אֲנִי מְחַכֶּה לָאוֹטוֹבּוּס – ani mekhake la-otobus – I am waiting for the bus
- אֲנִי עוֹזֵר לְחָבֵר – ani ozer le-khaver – I help a friend
- אֲנִי מְדַבֵּר בְּעִבְרִית – ani medaber be-ivrit – I speak Hebrew

Typical calques are לְחַכּוֹת עַל... and לַעֲזֹר אֶת.... It is better to learn such patterns from the start as ready-made collocations.

## “I want you to ...”: a different Hebrew construction

The Ukrainian “I want you to come” does not translate word for word. After רוצה, do not use an infinitive when the subordinate clause has a different subject. Instead, use שֶׁ + future tense.

Correct patterns:

- אֲנִי רוֹצֶה שֶׁתָּבוֹא – ani rotse she-tavo – I want you to come (to a man)
- אֲנִי רוֹצָה שֶׁתָּבוֹאִי – ani rotsa she-tavo'i – I want you to come (to a woman)
- אֲנִי רוֹצֶה שֶׁהוּא יִכְתֹּב – ani rotse she-hu yikhtov – I want him to write
- אֲנַחְנוּ צְרִיכִים שֶׁהֵם יָבִינוּ – anakhnu tsrikhim she-hem yavinu – we need them to understand

Calques such as אֲנִי רוֹצֶה לְךָ לָבוֹא or אֲנִי רוֹצֶה אוֹתְךָ לָבוֹא sound unnatural.

The infinitive after רוצה / יכול / צריך works only when the subject is the same: אֲנִי רוֹצֶה לָבוֹא – I want to come (I am the one who will come).

## Learn a ready-made pattern, not an isolated word

Many mistakes arise when someone first translates individual words and only then assembles a Ukrainian-style sentence. It is more reliable to remember short ready-made patterns:

- אֵין לִי זְמַן – I do not have time
- אֲנִי בֶּן עֶשְׂרִים – I am twenty years old
- אַתְּ רוֹצָה קָפֶה? – do you want coffee?
- אֲנִי מְחַכֶּה לָאוֹטוֹבּוּס – I am waiting for the bus
- אֲנִי רוֹצֶה שֶׁתָּבוֹא – I want you to come

## What to check in a sentence

Before remembering or repeating a phrase, quickly check:

- is the article ה־ needed?
- is the negation correct (לא / אין)?
- does the form agree in gender?
- is the preposition natural?
- are there different subjects in the main and subordinate clauses (for רוצה / צריך + ש)?
- has the whole construction been copied from Ukrainian?

Once this short internal check becomes a habit, the number of typical mistakes falls very quickly.
