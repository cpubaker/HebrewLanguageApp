# Smikhut: the construct state

Smikhut is a way of joining two nouns so that they form one complete meaning: a teacher’s book, a Hebrew lesson, the door of a house. English usually expresses this relationship with “of,” a possessive, or a compound noun; Hebrew simply places two nouns next to each other.

In smikhut:

- two nouns stand next to each other;
- the second word specifies the first: whose it is, what it concerns, or what it is for;
- the first word often changes form and sounds different from its dictionary form.

## Basic examples

- סֵפֶר מוֹרֶה – sefer more – a teacher’s book
- שִׁיעוּר עִבְרִית – shiur ivrit – a Hebrew lesson
- חֶדֶר יְלָדִים – kheder yeladim – a children’s room; a room for children
- בֵּית סֵפֶר – beit sefer – school

## How the first word changes

The second word in smikhut usually sounds as it does in the dictionary. The first often takes a special linked form. The change depends on the gender and number of the first word.

### Masculine singular

The first word may change very little, or it may change its vowels noticeably.

- סֵפֶר מוֹרֶה – sefer more – a teacher’s book
- חֶדֶר יְלָדִים – kheder yeladim – a children’s room

The clearest example is בַּיִת → בֵּית:

- בַּיִת – bayit – house
- בֵּית סֵפֶר – beit sefer – school

### Feminine singular

Words ending in ה often change to a form ending in ת:

- מִשְׁפָּחָה – mishpakha – family
- מִשְׁפַּחַת יֶלֶד – mishpakhat yeled – a boy’s family

- שָׁנָה – shana – year
- שְׁנַת לִמּוּד – shnat limud – an academic year

- מְדִינָה – medina – state, country
- מְדִינַת יִשְׂרָאֵל – medinat Yisrael – the State of Israel

In feminine singular forms, notice not only the final letter but the change in the whole sound: מִשְׁפָּחָה → מִשְׁפַּחַת, שָׁנָה → שְׁנַת, מְדִינָה → מְדִינַת.

### Masculine plural

Words ending in ־ים very often change to a form ending in ־י in smikhut:

- יְלָדִים – yeladim – children
- יַלְדֵי הַכִּתָּה – yaldei ha-kita – the children of the class

- סְפָרִים – sfarim – books
- סִפְרֵי יְלָדִים – sifrei yeladim – children’s books; books for children

This is one of the most useful patterns for recognizing smikhut in a text.

### Feminine plural

In writing, the ending ־ות often stays the same, although the pronunciation may change slightly:

- שְׁאֵלוֹת – she’elot – questions
- שְׁאֵלוֹת תַּלְמִידִים – she’elot talmidim – students’ questions

- דְּלָתוֹת – dlatot – doors
- דַּלְתוֹת הַבַּיִת – daltot ha-bayit – the doors of the house

The important point is not to look for a separate new ending, but to become familiar with complete forms inside phrases.

## Definiteness in smikhut

When the whole phrase is definite, the article ה appears on the second word, not the first.

- דֶּלֶת בַּיִת – delet bayit – a house door
- דֶּלֶת הַבַּיִת – delet ha-bayit – the door of the house

- רֹאשׁ עִיר – rosh ir – head of a city
- רֹאשׁ הָעִיר – rosh ha-ir – the mayor; head of the city

## Smikhut and an adjective are not the same

This is one of the most important distinctions. In similar-looking pairs, the second word can be either an adjective or a noun:

- סֵפֶר טוֹב – sefer tov – a good book (the second word is an adjective)
- סֵפֶר יְלָדִים – sefer yeladim – a children’s book; a book for children (the second word is a noun, so this is smikhut)

## Common patterns

- בֵּית סֵפֶר – school
- בֵּית חוֹלִים – hospital
- שִׁיעוּר עִבְרִית – Hebrew lesson
- סֵפֶר יְלָדִים – children’s book
- דֶּלֶת הַבַּיִת – the door of the house
- מְדִינַת יִשְׂרָאֵל – the State of Israel
- סִפְרֵי יְלָדִים – children’s books

## Common mistakes

- Confusing smikhut with a noun-plus-adjective phrase. In סֵפֶר טוֹב the second word is an adjective; in סֵפֶר יְלָדִים it is a noun in smikhut.
- Expecting the article on the first word. In smikhut, definiteness is normally marked on the second word: דֶּלֶת הַבַּיִת.
- Translating word by word too mechanically. שִׁיעוּר עִבְרִית is “a Hebrew lesson,” not “Hebrew lesson” in Hebrew word order. בֵּית חוֹלִים is “hospital,” not literally a “house of sick people” in ordinary English.
- Reading the first word as if it stood alone. In smikhut it often changes form: בַּיִת → בֵּית, מִשְׁפָּחָה → מִשְׁפַּחַת, יְלָדִים → יַלְדֵי.
