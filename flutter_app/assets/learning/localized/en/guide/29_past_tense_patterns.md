# Past tense: forms and usage

The introductory section on the past tense gives a first clue: the learner sees that an action has already happened and that the form depends on who performed it. This section takes the next step: seeing not separate examples, but the patterns that recur in many verbs.

The important thing is not to try to take in the whole table at once, but to notice calmly which pieces of a form repeat again and again. After that, the past tense becomes much easier to read.

In the past tense, it is useful to read a verb through two things:

- the word stem;
- the ending that shows person, gender, and number.

That is why the past tense often seems more table-like than the present. But once your eye is used to a few typical endings, this table-like quality no longer overwhelms; it helps.

## The most important things to notice

For practical reading, these endings are especially useful to recognize quickly:

- ־תִּי – most often “I”;
- ־תָּ / ־תְּ – “you, masculine” / “you, feminine”;
- a short base form without such an ending often means “he”;
- ־ָה – often means “she”;
- ־נוּ – often means “we”;
- ־תֶּם / ־תֶּן – “you” (masculine / feminine plural);
- ־וּ – often means “they.”

This is not the only possible pattern for every verb, but it works as a simple clue for many frequent words.

## One useful pattern

This is clear with the verb לִכְתֹּב – to write:

- כָּתַבְתִּי – katavti – I wrote
- כָּתַבְתָּ – katavta – you wrote (to a man)
- כָּתַבְתְּ – katavt – you wrote (to a woman)
- כָּתַב – katav – he wrote
- כָּתְבָה – katva – she wrote
- כָּתַבְנוּ – katavnu – we wrote
- כְּתַבְתֶּם – ktavtem – you wrote (masculine plural or a mixed group)
- כְּתַבְתֶּן – ktavten – you wrote (feminine plural)
- כָּתְבוּ – katvu – they wrote

Here the word stem stays visible while the ending changes first of all. This kind of repetition creates the sense of a system.

## The same pattern with another verb

### לִלְמֹד – to study; to learn

- לָמַדְתִּי – lamadti – I studied
- לָמַדְתָּ – lamadta – you studied (to a man)
- לָמַדְתְּ – lamadt – you studied (to a woman)
- לָמַד – lamad – he studied
- לָמְדָה – lamda – she studied
- לָמַדְנוּ – lamadnu – we studied
- לְמַדְתֶּם – lemadtem – you studied (masculine plural or a mixed group)
- לְמַדְתֶּן – lemadten – you studied (feminine plural)
- לָמְדוּ – lamdu – they studied

When this pattern recurs in several familiar words, the past tense stops looking random.

## A verb group with a changing stem

With verbs such as לִקְנוֹת, לִשְׁתּוֹת, and לִרְצוֹת, the past tense uses a stem with יוֹד in some forms.

### לִקְנוֹת – to buy

- קָנִיתִי – kaniti – I bought
- קָנִיתָ – kanita – you bought (to a man)
- קָנִית – kanit – you bought (to a woman)
- קָנָה – kana – he bought
- קָנְתָה – kanta – she bought
- קָנִינוּ – kaninu – we bought
- קָנוּ – kanu – they bought

What this shares with the basic pattern: the personal endings remain the same. What is special: the stem has י in the “I / you / we” forms.

## The past tense in פִּעֵל

In פִּעֵל (and other binyanim), the past tense has the same personal endings too; only the stem pattern itself changes.

### לְדַבֵּר – to speak

- דִּבַּרְתִּי – dibarti – I spoke
- דִּבַּרְתָּ – dibarta – you spoke (to a man)
- דִּבַּרְתְּ – dibart – you spoke (to a woman)
- דִּבֵּר – diber – he spoke
- דִּבְּרָה – dibra – she spoke
- דִּבַּרְנוּ – dibarnu – we spoke
- דִּבְּרוּ – dibru – they spoke

The personal endings are easy to recognize, while the characteristic doubling (the dagesh in the middle root consonant) is a sign of the פיעל binyan.

## The second person: the most common point of confusion

The most common early mistake is failing to distinguish the two “you” forms:

- ־תָּ – usually when speaking to a man;
- ־תְּ – usually when speaking to a woman.

Compare:

- כָּתַבְתָּ – you wrote (to a man)
- כָּתַבְתְּ – you wrote (to a woman)
- הָיִיתָ – you were (to a man)
- הָיִיתְ – you were (to a woman)

This pair causes difficulty very often in real reading, so it is worth learning as a distinct pair.

## The third person: short and longer forms

For many frequent verbs, the “he” form is shorter and the “she” form has a more distinct ending.

- כָּתַב – he wrote
- כָּתְבָה – she wrote
- לָמַד – he studied
- לָמְדָה – she studied
- עָבַד – he worked
- עָבְדָה – she worked

This pair is also very useful for rapid recognition.

## Plural

At the start, the three most useful plural forms to recognize are:

- ־נוּ – we
- ־תֶּם / ־תֶּן – you
- ־וּ – they

Examples:

- כָּתַבְנוּ – we wrote
- לָמַדְנוּ – we studied
- הָיִינוּ – we were
- כְּתַבְתֶּם – you wrote (masculine plural or a mixed group)
- כְּתַבְתֶּן – you wrote (feminine plural)
- כָּתְבוּ – they wrote
- לָמְדוּ – they studied
- הָיוּ – they were

## Not every verb is equally transparent

Even when the general pattern works, the most common verbs can still have forms that are best memorized separately.

This applies especially to:

- הָיָה – to be
- הָלַךְ – to go
- בָּא – to come
- נָתַן – to give

The pattern is not meant to replace vocabulary memory, but to make it more organized.

## How to learn the past tense without overload

A useful order is:

1. see “I,” “he,” and “she”;
2. consolidate the “you, masculine” / “you, feminine” pair separately;
3. add “we” and “they”;
4. then add “you” plural;
5. only after that, apply the pattern to new verbs.

This approach is more useful than trying to memorize a full table for twenty words immediately.

## Common mistakes

- Learning every verb as if from scratch. If a learner does not see the endings, then כָּתַבְתִּי and לָמַדְתִּי look like two completely different forms. In fact, both already show the same “I” pattern.
- Not noticing the ־תָּ / ־תְּ pair. The second person is often what undermines confidence in reading. It is better to practice it separately and regularly.
- Thinking a pattern eliminates exceptions. A pattern helps, but does not remove the need to recognize frequent irregular verbs separately.
- Expecting verbs such as לִקְנוֹת to behave exactly like כָּתַב. They have a י in the stem (קָנִיתִי, not *קָנַתְתִּי).
