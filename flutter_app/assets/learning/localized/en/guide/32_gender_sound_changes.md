# How gender changes the sound of a word

In many words, moving between masculine and feminine changes not only the ending but also the sound of the word itself. This is especially noticeable in adjectives, participial forms, and some common nouns.

That is why it is important not to reduce everything to the formula “add a feminine ending.” It is often more useful to hear and see a pair right away as two related, but not completely identical, words.

When gender changes, look not only at the end of the word but at its whole sound pattern:

- sometimes only a feminine ending is added;
- sometimes the stress changes;
- sometimes one of the vowels weakens or disappears;
- sometimes the spelling is almost the same while the pronunciation is already different.

If you notice these four possibilities from the start, many forms stop being surprising in reading and listening.

## A shift in stress

Hebrew adjectives usually have stress on the final syllable. When the feminine ending ־ָה is added to a masculine form, the new syllable takes the stress; the previous syllable weakens and often loses its vowel.

A simple example:

- קָטָן – ka-TAN – small (stress on “tan”)
- קְטַנָּה – kta-NA – small (feminine; stress on “na,” and the vowel in the first syllable has reduced to a sheva)

This stress shift and reduction of the first vowel are among the most characteristic changes when adjectives move to the feminine form.

## When the feminine form sounds shorter

Because of the stress shift, the feminine form often seems to compress an internal vowel.

- קָטָן – katan – small
- קְטַנָּה – ktana – small (feminine)

- גָּדוֹל – gadol – big
- גְּדוֹלָה – gdola – big (feminine)

- קָצָר – katsar – short
- קְצָרָה – ktsara – short (feminine)

- חָדָשׁ – khadash – new
- חֲדָשָׁה – khadasha – new (feminine)

## When spelling is almost the same but reading differs

Some masculine and feminine forms look identical or nearly identical without niqqud, but are pronounced differently. For a beginner, this is one of the places where pronunciation becomes just as important as spelling.

- יָפֶה – yafe – handsome; beautiful
- יָפָה – yafa – beautiful (feminine)

- קָשֶׁה – kashe – difficult; hard
- קָשָׁה – kasha – difficult; hard (feminine)

- מוֹרֶה – more – teacher (male)
- מוֹרָה – mora – teacher (female)

This is one reason why learning only Hebrew spelling without pronunciation is risky.

## When the change is quite transparent

Not all words change sharply. In many cases, the feminine form looks like a fairly transparent addition of an ending.

- טוֹב – tov – good
- טוֹבָה – tova – good (feminine)
- חָכָם – khakham – smart
- חֲכָמָה – khakhama – smart (feminine)
- תַּלְמִיד – talmid – student (male)
- תַּלְמִידָה – talmida – student (female)
- חַם – kham – hot
- חַמָּה – khama – hot (feminine)

## The same happens with nouns

Not only adjectives can change noticeably in sound. Nouns also often show that a feminine form is sometimes built transparently and sometimes sounds less predictable.

Simpler pairs:

- דֹּב – dov – bear
- דֻּבָּה – duba – female bear
- חָתוּל – khatul – cat (male)
- חֲתוּלָה – khatula – cat (female)
- תַּלְמִיד – talmid – student (male)
- תַּלְמִידָה – talmida – student (female)

Pairs with a stronger sound change:

- מֶלֶךְ – melekh – king
- מַלְכָּה – malka – queen

- נָסִיךְ – nasikh – prince
- נְסִיכָה – nesikha – princess

- חָבֵר – khaver – friend (male)
- חֲבֵרָה – khavera – friend (female)

## What to pay attention to

When you see a masculine / feminine pair, it is useful to check:

- whether only the ending changed;
- where the stress moved;
- whether an internal vowel disappeared or weakened;
- whether the word looks the same without niqqud.

This quick check helps you do more than merely memorize a form: it helps you notice how the word behaves in the language.

## Useful pairs

These forms are best learned immediately as pairs:

- קָטָן / קְטַנָּה – small (masculine) / small (feminine)
- גָּדוֹל / גְּדוֹלָה – big (masculine) / big (feminine)
- יָפֶה / יָפָה – beautiful (masculine) / beautiful (feminine)
- טוֹב / טוֹבָה – good (masculine) / good (feminine)
- חָדָשׁ / חֲדָשָׁה – new (masculine) / new (feminine)
- חַם / חַמָּה – hot (masculine) / hot (feminine)
- דֹּב / דֻּבָּה – bear / female bear
- מֶלֶךְ / מַלְכָּה – king / queen
- מוֹרֶה / מוֹרָה – teacher (male) / teacher (female)

## Common mistakes

- Looking only at the last letter. In gender changes, not only the ending matters, but the whole sound pattern of the word, especially the stress position.
- Expecting the feminine form always to be simply “word + a.” In many words, the feminine form restructures the rhythm: קָטָן → קְטַנָּה, גָּדוֹל → גְּדוֹלָה, מֶלֶךְ → מַלְכָּה.
- Learning only one form without its pair. With words such as יפה / יפה or מורה / מורה, one form is hard to read correctly without niqqud if you do not know the other. It is better to remember both straight away.
- Not noticing stress. Stress is not marked in writing, but in speech it is often the only noticeable signal that a word is feminine.
