# Adverbs of Manner

Hebrew does not have one general ending like English *-ly*. Instead, it uses several different patterns in which the construction itself, rather than a word ending, does the main work. As a result, the question “how?” is often conveyed not by one word but by a short, ready-made phrase.

This topic is useful because it removes uncertainty: when Hebrew seems to lack a quick adverb ending, it is really offering a different pattern. Once you recognise these patterns, your phrasing becomes more natural.

In modern Hebrew, adverbs of manner are formed mainly in the following ways:

- **בְּ** + an abstract noun: **בְּשֶׁקֶט** (quietly), **בְּקַלּוּת** (easily);
- a short adjective form on its own: **מַהֵר** (quickly), **טוֹב** (well);
- **בְּלִי** + a noun or infinitive: **בְּלִי שְׁאֵלוֹת** (without questions), **בְּלִי לַחֲשֹׁב** (without thinking);
- fixed expressions such as **בְּדִיּוּק**, **בְּכַוָּנָה**, and **בְּטָעוּת**.

## The most productive pattern: ב + a noun

This is the main way to form an adverb of manner from an adjective. Take an abstract noun with the same root and put it after the preposition **ב**.

- שֶׁקֶט – sheket – quiet; silence → בְּשֶׁקֶט – be-sheket – quietly
- קַלּוּת – kalut – ease → בְּקַלּוּת – be-kalut – easily
- זְהִירוּת – zehirut – caution → בִּזְהִירוּת – bi-zehirut – carefully
- מְהִירוּת – mehirut – speed → בִּמְהִירוּת – bi-mehirut – quickly
- שָׂמְחָה – simcha – joy → בְּשִׂמְחָה – be-simcha – joyfully
- כֹּחַ – koach – strength → בְּכֹחַ – be-koach – forcefully; with strength
- רְצִינוּת – retsinut – seriousness → בִּרְצִינוּת – bi-retsinut – seriously
- כַּעַס – ka'as – anger → בְּכַעַס – be-ka'as – angrily
- עֲדִינוּת – adinut – gentleness → בַּעֲדִינוּת – ba-adinut – gently

Examples in sentences:

- הוּא דִּבֵּר בְּשֶׁקֶט – hu diber be-sheket – he spoke quietly
- הִיא נִכְנְסָה בִּזְהִירוּת – hi niknesa bi-zehirut – she entered carefully
- אֲנַחְנוּ עוֹבְדִים בִּרְצִינוּת – anachnu ovdim bi-retsinut – we work seriously
- הַיֶּלֶד יָשֵׁן בְּעֲדִינוּת – ha-yeled yashen be-adinut – the child sleeps softly

This is the most flexible pattern: almost any abstract noun expressing a quality can be used in this way.

## An adjective used as an adverb: the short form

Some words have the same form when used as an adjective and as an adverb of manner. These are usually short, very common words.

- מַהֵר – maher – quickly
- לְאַט – le'at – slowly
- טוֹב – tov – well
- רַע – ra – badly
- חָזָק – chazak – strongly
- חַלָּשׁ – chalash – weakly
- יָפֶה – yafe – beautifully; nicely

Examples:

- הוּא רָץ מַהֵר – hu rats maher – he runs quickly
- דַּבְּרוּ לְאַט, בְּבַקָּשָׁה – dabru le'at, be-vakasha – please speak slowly
- הוּא יָשֵׁן טוֹב – hu yashen tov – he sleeps well
- אֲנִי מַרְגִּישׁ רַע הַיּוֹם – ani margish ra ha-yom – I feel bad today
- הִיא שָׁרָה יָפֶה – hi shara yafe – she sings beautifully

Notice that these forms do not change for gender or number when they function as adverbs. In **הִיא רָצָה מַהֵר** (“she ran quickly”), **מַהֵר** stays in the same form.

## בלי + a noun or infinitive

This is a very productive pattern meaning “without something” or “without doing something.”

- בְּלִי שְׁאֵלוֹת – bli she'elot – without questions
- בְּלִי כַּוָּנָה – bli kavana – without intending to; unintentionally
- בְּלִי הֲפוּגָה – bli hafuga – without a break
- בְּלִי לַחֲשֹׁב – bli lachshov – without thinking
- בְּלִי לְהָבִין – bli lehavin – without understanding
- בְּלִי לוֹמַר מִלָּה – bli lomar mila – without saying a word

Examples:

- הוּא יָצָא בְּלִי לְהַגִּיד שָׁלוֹם – hu yatsa bli lehagid shalom – he left without saying goodbye
- הִיא הִסְכִּימָה בְּלִי שְׁאֵלוֹת – hi hiskima bli she'elot – she agreed without asking questions
- אֲנִי עוֹשֶׂה אֶת זֶה בְּלִי כַּוָּנָה – ani ose et ze bli kavana – I do this unintentionally

## Fixed expressions

Some adverbial phrases are so common that it is best to learn them as complete units.

- בְּדִיּוּק – be-diyuk – exactly
- בְּכַוָּנָה – be-kavana – deliberately; on purpose
- בְּטָעוּת – be-ta'ut – by mistake
- בְּפֹעַל – be-fo'al – in practice; actually
- בְּסוֹף – be-sof – at the end
- בְּאֱמֶת – be-emet – really; truly
- מֵרָצוֹן – me-ratson – voluntarily
- בְּמַזָּל – be-mazal – fortunately
- לְמַרְבֵּה הַצַּעַר – le-marbe ha-tsa'ar – unfortunately
- לְמַרְבֵּה הַשִּׂמְחָה – le-marbe ha-simcha – fortunately; happily

Examples:

- הוּא הִתְקַשֵּׁר בְּדִיּוּק כְּשֶׁיָּצָאתִי – hu hitkasher be-diyuk ke-she-yatsati – he called exactly when I was leaving
- הִיא לֹא עָשְׂתָה זֹאת בְּכַוָּנָה – hi lo aseta zot be-kavana – she did not do that on purpose
- בְּמַזָּל הִגַּעְתִּי בַּזְּמַן – be-mazal higa'ti ba-zman – fortunately, I arrived on time
- לְמַרְבֵּה הַצַּעַר, הַחֲנוּת סְגוּרָה – le-marbe ha-tsa'ar, ha-chanut sgura – unfortunately, the shop is closed

## How it sounds in everyday speech

There is often more than one adverb of manner in a sentence:

- הוּא דִּבֵּר בְּשֶׁקֶט וּבִזְהִירוּת – hu diber be-sheket u-vi-zehirut – he spoke quietly and carefully
- אֲנַחְנוּ עוֹשִׂים זֹאת מַהֵר וּבְלִי הֲפוּגָה – anachnu osim zot maher u-vli hafuga – we are doing this quickly and without a break
- הִיא הִגִּיבָה בְּעֲדִינוּת, אֲבָל בְּרָצוֹן – hi higi'a be-adinut, aval be-ratson – she responded gently, but willingly

An adverb usually comes after the verb, not at the start of the sentence. Moving it to the beginning makes it the focus (“quietly in particular,” “quickly in particular”) and sounds emphatic.

## Compare

- הוּא רָץ – hu rats – he ran
- הוּא רָץ מַהֵר – hu rats maher – he ran quickly
- הוּא רָץ בִּמְהִירוּת – hu rats bi-mehirut – he ran at speed

- הִיא דִּבְּרָה – hi dibra – she spoke
- הִיא דִּבְּרָה בְּשֶׁקֶט – hi dibra be-sheket – she spoke quietly
- הִיא דִּבְּרָה בְּלִי לְהַפְסִיק – hi dibra bli lehafsik – she spoke without stopping

- אֲנִי עוֹבֵד – ani oved – I work
- אֲנִי עוֹבֵד בִּרְצִינוּת – ani oved bi-retsinut – I work seriously
- אֲנִי עוֹבֵד טוֹב – ani oved tov – I work well

## Common mistakes

- Looking for one “adverb ending.” Hebrew does not have one: it has several patterns—**ב** + a noun, a short adjective form, and **בלי** + an infinitive. Together they cover the meanings expressed by English “how?” and *-ly* adverbs.
- Making an adverb agree with the subject in gender. An adverb of manner does not change: **הִיא רָצָה מַהֵר**, not *הִיא רָצָה מְהִירָה*.
- Putting an adverb only at the beginning of a sentence. In neutral order, it follows the verb. At the beginning, it becomes an emphasis.
- Translating “quickly” literally as **מָהִיר**. **מָהִיר** is the adjective “fast” (**אוטו מהיר** – “a fast car”). To mean “quickly” with a verb, use **מַהֵר** or **בִּמְהִירוּת**.
- Confusing **בלי** + a noun with **בלי** + an infinitive. The first means “without something” (**בלי סבלנות** – without patience); the second means “without doing something” (**בלי לחשוב** – without thinking). Although both use “without” in English, the infinitive construction more often conveys a process.
