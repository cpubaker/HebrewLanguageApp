# Reported Speech

When you do not quote someone’s words directly but report them in your own words, Hebrew uses distinct patterns. It is not enough simply to say “he said something and then something else”: the form used to convey another person’s speech matters. Is it a statement, a question, or a wish, and how do the tenses behave?

For an English-speaking learner, the main feature is that tenses in the subordinate clause do not shift as they do in English. What was said remains in the tense in which the speaker originally viewed it.

Reported speech most often occurs in three situations:

- reporting a statement: “he said that...”;
- reporting a question: “she asked whether...” or “she asked where...”;
- reporting a wish or request: “she wants [someone] to...”.

In practice, the following patterns are the most useful:

- אָמַר שֶׁ... – amar she... – said that...
- סִפֵּר שֶׁ... – siper she... – told [someone] that...
- צִיֵּן שֶׁ... – tsiyen she... – noted that...
- שָׁאַל אִם... – sha'al im... – asked whether...
- שָׁאַל מָה / מִי / אֵיפֹה... – sha'al ma / mi / eifo... – asked what / who / where...
- בִּקֵּשׁ שֶׁ... – bikesh she... – asked [someone] to... + future tense
- רוֹצֶה שֶׁ... – rotse she... – wants [someone] to... + future tense

## Reporting a statement: ש + a clause

After a verb of speech, use **ש** and then a clause with its own subject and complete meaning.

- הוּא אָמַר שֶׁהוּא יָבוֹא מָחָר – hu amar she-hu yavo machar – he said that he would come tomorrow
- הִיא סִפְּרָה שֶׁהֵם גָּרִים בִּירוּשָׁלַיִם – hi sipra she-hem garim bi-Yerushalayim – she said that they live in Jerusalem
- דָּנָה צִיְּנָה שֶׁהַתָּכְנִית הִשְׁתַּנְּתָה – Dana tsiyena she-ha-tokhnit hishtanta – Dana noted that the plan had changed
- הוּא הוֹדִיעַ שֶׁהוּא לֹא יַגִּיעַ – hu hodi'a she-hu lo yagi'a – he announced that he would not be able to come

In the future tense, the verb form already encodes the person, so **שֶׁיָּבוֹא** is also possible: “that he will come.” Add an explicit pronoun, as in **שֶׁהוּא יָבוֹא**, when the subject needs emphasis or clarification—for example, when several people could be meant in the conversation.

## Tenses stay in place rather than shifting

This is the biggest difference from English, and a common trap for learners who use an English framework.

English: *He said he would come* (`would` because `said` is in the past).<br>
Hebrew: הוּא אָמַר שֶׁיָּבוֹא – literally, “he said that he will come.”

In other words, the tense in the subordinate clause is the one the original speaker used or had in mind at the time of speaking.

Examples:

- חָשַׁבְתִּי שֶׁאַתָּה בַּבַּיִת – chashavti she-ata ba-bayit – I thought you were at home (not “had been at home” if you are there now)
- הִיא אָמְרָה שֶׁהִיא לוֹמֶדֶת עִבְרִית – hi amra she-hi lomedet ivrit – she said that she is studying Hebrew
- הוּא אָמַר שֶׁהוּא הָיָה שָׁם אֶתְמוֹל – hu amar she-hu haya sham etmol – he said that he was there yesterday
- אָמַרְתִּי שֶׁאַסְפִּיק בַּזְּמַן – amarti she-aspik ba-zman – I said that I would finish on time

In the last example, the subordinate clause is in the future even though the main clause is in the past. That is normal in Hebrew.

## Yes/no questions: אם

Report a yes/no question with **אִם**.

- הִיא שָׁאֲלָה אִם אֲנִי בָּא – hi sha'ala im ani ba – she asked whether I was coming
- הוּא בָּדַק אִם הַחֲנוּת פְּתוּחָה – hu badak im ha-chanut ptucha – he checked whether the shop was open
- אֲנִי לֹא יוֹדֵעַ אִם הִיא הִסְכִּימָה – ani lo yode'a im hi hiskima – I do not know whether she agreed

Do not confuse it with interrogative **הַאִם** (formal “whether” in a direct question) or conditional **אִם** (“if”). In an indirect question, **אִם** means “whether.”

## Questions with a question word

If the direct question contained **מָה / מִי / אֵיפֹה / מָתַי / לָמָּה / כַּמָּה**, that word remains in reported speech, but the word order becomes declarative.

- שָׁאַלְתִּי מָתַי הִיא מַגִּיעָה – sha'alti matai hi magi'a – I asked when she was arriving
- הוּא לֹא יוֹדֵעַ אֵיפֹה הַמַּפְתֵּחוֹת – hu lo yode'a eifo ha-mafteachot – he does not know where the keys are
- תָּגִּיד לִי לָמָּה זֶה קָרָה – tagid li lama ze kara – tell me why this happened
- שָׁאַלְתִּי מִי הוּא – sha'alti mi hu – I asked who he was

Verbs of knowledge and uncertainty (**יוֹדֵעַ**, **זוֹכֵר**, **בָּטוּחַ**) also frequently introduce this kind of indirect construction:

- אֲנִי לֹא זוֹכֵר כַּמָּה זֶה עָלָה – ani lo zokher kama ze ala – I do not remember how much it cost
- אֲנִי בָּטוּחַ אֵיפֹה זֶה – ani batu'ach eifo ze – I am sure where it is

## Requests and wishes: ש + future

When a speaker wants someone **else** to do something, a verb such as **ביקש**, **רוצה**, or **מצפה** is followed by **ש** + the future tense.

- בִּקַּשְׁתִּי שֶׁיַּחְזֹר אֵלַי – bikashti she-yachzor elai – I asked him to call me back
- הִיא רוֹצָה שֶׁתָּבוֹא בַּזְּמַן – hi rotsa she-tavo ba-zman – she wants you to come on time
- מְצַפִּים שֶׁנַּגִּיעַ עַד שָׁעָה שֵׁשׁ – metsapim she-nagi'a ad sha'a shesh – they expect us to arrive by six o’clock
- הַמּוֹרָה דּוֹרֶשֶׁת שֶׁכֻּלָּם יִקְרְאוּ אֶת הַטֶּקְסְט – ha-mora doreshet she-kulam yikre'u et ha-tekst – the teacher requires everyone to read the text

If the speaker wants to do something **themself**, use an infinitive instead of **ש** + future:

- אֲנִי רוֹצֶה לָבוֹא – ani rotse lavo – I want to come (I am the one who will come)
- אֲנִי רוֹצֶה שֶׁתָּבוֹא – ani rotse she-tavo – I want you to come (a different subject)

## Changing pronouns

When quoted speech becomes reported speech, pronouns shift according to who is speaking.

Direct speech: דָּנָה אָמְרָה לִי: “אֲנִי לֹא יְכוֹלָה לָבוֹא”.<br>
Reported speech: דָּנָה אָמְרָה לִי שֶׁהִיא לֹא יְכוֹלָה לָבוֹא.

Direct speech: יוֹסִי שָׁאַל אוֹתִי: “אַתָּה רוֹצֶה תֵּה?”.<br>
Reported speech: יוֹסִי שָׁאַל אוֹתִי אִם אֲנִי רוֹצֶה תֵּה.

The logic is the same as in English: the quoted person’s “I” becomes “he” or “she,” “you” can become “I,” and so on.

## How it sounds in everyday speech

In conversation, reported speech most often uses simple constructions:

- הוּא אָמַר שֶׁ...
- הִיא סִפְּרָה שֶׁ...
- שָׁאַלְתִּי אִם...
- בִּקַּשְׁתִּי שֶׁ...
- אֲנִי לֹא יוֹדֵעַ אִם / מָתַי / אֵיפֹה...

These patterns cover most everyday situations.

## Common mistakes

- Shifting the subordinate-clause tense as in English. In Hebrew, **הוּא אָמַר שֶׁיָּבוֹא** literally means “he said that he will come,” not *הוּא אָמַר שֶׁהָיָה בָּא*.
- Treating the pronoun in the subordinate clause as required. In the future tense, **הוּא אָמַר שֶׁיָּבוֹא** is possible. **הוּא אָמַר שֶׁהוּא יָבוֹא** is also correct and is useful for emphasis or for clarifying the subject.
- Confusing **אִם** (“whether” in an indirect question) with **אִם** (“if” in a condition). **שָׁאַלְתִּי אִם אַתָּה בָּא** means “I asked whether you were coming.” **אִם אַתָּה בָּא, נֵצֵא** means “if you come, we will leave.” Context and the verb of the main clause usually remove the ambiguity.
- Using an infinitive after **רוצה / מבקש** when the subject is different. **אֲנִי רוֹצֶה לָבוֹא** means “I want to come” (I will come). But if I want someone else to come, say **אֲנִי רוֹצֶה שֶׁתָּבוֹא**, not *אֲנִי רוֹצֶה לְךָ לָבוֹא*.
- Keeping direct-question word order in an indirect question. Direct: **מָתַי אַתָּה בָּא?** Indirect: **שָׁאַלְתִּי מָתַי אַתָּה בָּא**—the question word begins the subordinate clause, with no inversion of word order.
