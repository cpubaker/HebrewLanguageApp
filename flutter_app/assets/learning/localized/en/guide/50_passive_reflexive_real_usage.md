# Passive and reflexive forms

Not every action is presented in Hebrew in the same way. Sometimes the person doing it matters: “someone opened the door.” At other times, the result or state matters more: “the door opened,” “the shop closed,” “I was invited.” There are also reflexive patterns such as “to get dressed,” “to get ready,” and “to wash oneself.” It is best to see these as living patterns of speech, not only as names of binyanim.

This topic often becomes clearer when you shift the focus instead of starting with terminology: who matters in the sentence—the person doing the action, or the result that happened to a person or thing?

In practice, three situations are especially frequent:

- an action happened, but the person doing it is not named or is not important;
- the result for an object or person is important;
- a person does something to themselves or enters a certain state.

The most useful distinctions are:

- change of state or “it happened”: נִפְעַל (נפתח, נסגר, נשבר);
- clear passive: פֻּעַל / הֻפְעַל (הוזמן, פורסם, תורגם);
- reflexivity or an action on oneself: הִתְפַּעֵל (התלבש, התקלח, התכונן).

## When the result matters, not the person doing it

Very often it is more natural to say not “someone did something,” but “something opened / closed / broke.”

- הַדֶּלֶת נִפְתְּחָה – ha-delet nifteha – the door opened
- הַחֲנוּת נִסְגְּרָה מֻקְדָּם – ha-khanut nisgera mukdam – the shop closed early
- הַכּוֹס נִשְׁבְּרָה – ha-kos nishbera – the glass broke
- הַיֶּלֶד נִרְדַּם – ha-yeled nirdam – the child fell asleep

In these sentences, the person who caused the action is unknown or simply unimportant to the idea.

In the present tense, the same patterns sound like this:

- הַדֶּלֶת נִפְתַּחַת – ha-delet niftakhat – the door is opening
- הַחֲנוּת נִסְגֶּרֶת בָּעֶרֶב – ha-khanut nisdgeret ba-erev – the shop closes in the evening

## When a passive is genuinely useful

Some patterns stress that something was done to a person or object. If the person doing it does matter, add them with על-ידי (“by”).

- הֻזְמַנְתִּי לִפְגִישָׁה – huzmanti la-pgisha – I was invited to a meeting
- הַמִּסְמָךְ תֻּרְגַּם לְעִבְרִית – ha-mismakh turgam le-ivrit – the document was translated into Hebrew
- הַמַּאֲמָר פֻּרְסַם אֶתְמוֹל – ha-ma'amar pursam etmol – the article was published yesterday
- הַכַּרְטִיסִים נִמְכְּרוּ מַהֵר – ha-kartisim nimkeru maher – the tickets sold quickly
- הַסֵּפֶר נִכְתַּב עַל־יְדֵי דָּנָה – ha-sefer nikhtav al-yedei Dana – the book was written by Dana

Some of these forms may be translated into English with a passive, while others are more natural as an active sentence with an unspecified subject.

## Reflexive actions

Here a person does something to themselves or enters a state.

- אֲנִי מִתְלַבֵּשׁ מַהֵר – ani mitlabesh maher – I get dressed quickly
- הִיא הִתְקַלְּחָה וְעַכְשָׁו הִיא מוּכָנָה – hi hitkalkha ve-akhshav hi mukhana – she took a shower and is ready now
- אֲנַחְנוּ מִתְכּוֹנְנִים לַנְּסִיעָה – anakhnu mitkonenim la-nesia – we are preparing for the trip
- הוּא הִסְתַּדֵּר לְבַד – hu histader levad – he managed on his own
- מָחָר אֶתְקַלֵּחַ מֻקְדָּם – makhar etkaleakh mukdam – tomorrow I will shower early

## Not everything translates with “self”

Many frequent הִתְפַּעֵל forms have an established meaning that cannot be reduced to the literal idea of “doing something to oneself.”

- הוּא מִתְלַבֵּשׁ – he is getting dressed
- הוּא מִתְכּוֹנֵן – he is preparing
- הוּא מִסְתַּדֵּר – he manages; he gets by
- הוּא הִתְחַתֵּן עִם דָּנָה – hu hitkhaten im Dana – he married Dana
- הִיא הִתְרַגְּלָה לַחֹם – hi hitragla la-khom – she got used to the heat
- אֲנִי מִתְעַנְיֵן בְּהִיסְטוֹרְיָה – ani mit'anyen be-historia – I am interested in history
- הוּא הִתְרַגֵּשׁ מֵהַחֲדָשׁוֹת – hu hitragesh me-ha-khadashot – he was moved by the news

All these sentences have a reflexive logic, but their natural English translations can be quite different.

## Compare an active action and a result

- מִישֶׁהוּ פָּתַח אֶת הַדֶּלֶת – someone opened the door
- הַדֶּלֶת נִפְתְּחָה – the door opened

- מִישֶׁהוּ סָגַר אֶת הַחֲנוּת – someone closed the shop
- הַחֲנוּת נִסְגְּרָה – the shop closed

- הֵם הִזְמִינוּ אוֹתִי – they invited me
- הֻזְמַנְתִּי – I was invited

In the second sentence, the focus is no longer on the person doing the action, but on its result.

## How to read these forms without overloading yourself with terms

In practice, do not start with binyan names. Ask three simple questions instead:

- does it matter who did the action?
- is the result or new state more important?
- is the person doing something to themselves or entering a state?

This makes it much easier to understand why the sentence uses that particular form.

## Common mistakes

- Looking for a named person doing the action everywhere. In natural speech that person is often not mentioned at all: הַחֲנוּת נִסְגְּרָה, הַכּוֹס נִשְׁבְּרָה.
- Translating reflexivity too literally. Do not mechanically look for “self” in every הִתְפַּעֵל form. הִתְחַתֵּן means “got married,” not “married himself”; הִתְעַנְיֵן means “was interested,” not “interested himself.”
- Being afraid of short passive forms. Phrases such as הֻזְמַנְתִּי, פֻּרְסַם, and נִמְכַּר are not necessarily overly formal; some are entirely ordinary in everyday Hebrew.
- Learning only the term rather than a ready example. It is better to remember הַדֶּלֶת נִפְתְּחָה, הֻזְמַנְתִּי לִפְגִישָׁה, and אֲנִי מִתְכּוֹנֵן than only names of form types without a living sentence.
- Not knowing how to attach the person doing the action in a passive. If you need to say by whom something was done, use על-ידי: הַמַּאֲמָר נִכְתַּב עַל־יְדֵי הַחוֹקֵר.
