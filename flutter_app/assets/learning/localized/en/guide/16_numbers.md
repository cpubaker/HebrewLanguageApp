# Numbers

Hebrew numbers quickly go beyond a simple list of “one, two, three.” Even at a basic level, two things matter: their form often depends on the gender of the noun, and some numbers look different before a noun than they do in a dictionary. Learning only a table can therefore make the topic feel chaotic.

Start with four anchors:

- “one” has separate forms: אֶחָד / אַחַת;
- “two” has special forms before a noun: שְׁנֵי / שְׁתֵּי;
- for numbers 3–10, the form also depends on the noun’s gender;
- the question כַּמָּה lets you build useful real-life phrases before the whole system becomes automatic.

It is best to learn a number as part of a phrase, not as an isolated list item. Each time, ask yourself two questions:

1. Does the number stand directly before a noun or on its own?
2. Is the noun masculine or feminine?

These two points usually explain why the form looks different from what you expect.

## Quick reference: 1–10

The first form goes with a masculine noun, the second with a feminine noun:

- 1: אֶחָד / אַחַת – ekhad / akhat
- 2: שְׁנֵי / שְׁתֵּי – shnei / shtei
- 3: שְׁלוֹשָׁה / שָׁלוֹשׁ – shlosha / shalosh
- 4: אַרְבָּעָה / אַרְבַּע – arba’a / arba
- 5: חֲמִשָּׁה / חָמֵשׁ – khamisha / khamesh
- 6: שִׁשָּׁה / שֵׁשׁ – shisha / shesh
- 7: שִׁבְעָה / שֶׁבַע – shiv’a / sheva
- 8: שְׁמוֹנָה / שְׁמוֹנֶה – shmona / shmone
- 9: תִּשְׁעָה / תֵּשַׁע – tish’a / tesha
- 10: עֲשָׂרָה / עֶשֶׂר – asara / eser

One special trap: with numbers 3–10, the masculine form ends in -ה, which can look feminine, while the feminine form does not. This is the well-known reversed gender pattern of Hebrew numbers.

## One

The logic for “one” is clear. אֶחָד / אַחַת comes after the noun and agrees with it in gender.

- סֵפֶר אֶחָד – sefer ekhad – one book
- מַחְבֶּרֶת אַחַת – makhberet akhat – one notebook
- יֶלֶד אֶחָד – yeled ekhad – one boy
- יַלְדָּה אַחַת – yalda akhat – one girl

## Two: an important special case

Many learners first stumble here because the dictionary form and the form before a noun differ.

When “two” stands before a noun, use שְׁנֵי / שְׁתֵּי:

- שְׁנֵי סְפָרִים – shnei sfarim – two books
- שְׁתֵּי מַחְבָּרוֹת – shtei makhbarot – two notebooks

When the number stands on its own—in counting or an answer—use שְׁנַיִם / שְׁתַּיִם:

- שְׁנַיִם – shnayim – two, two people
- שְׁתַּיִם – shtayim – two

Compare:

- יֵשׁ כָּאן שְׁנֵי סְפָרִים – yesh kan shnei sfarim – There are two books here
- יֵשׁ כָּאן שְׁתַּיִם – yesh kan shtayim – There are two here

## Three to ten: look at the phrase

For numbers 3–10, look not only at the number itself but also at the noun beside it. The number comes before the noun and agrees with its gender.

Masculine nouns:

- שְׁלוֹשָׁה תַּלְמִידִים – shlosha talmidim – three students
- אַרְבָּעָה סְפָרִים – arba’a sfarim – four books
- חֲמִשָּׁה יָמִים – khamisha yamim – five days

Feminine nouns:

- שָׁלוֹשׁ תַּלְמִידוֹת – shalosh talmidot – three female students
- אַרְבַּע מַחְבָּרוֹת – arba makhbarot – four notebooks
- חָמֵשׁ דַּקּוֹת – khamesh dakot – five minutes

The system becomes faster if you notice common ready-made combinations:

- with words such as ספרים, ילדים, ימים, you will more often see שלושה / ארבעה / חמישה;
- with nouns such as מחברת, דקה, תלמידה, you will see שלוש / ארבע / חמש.

## כמה: how many, how much

One of the most useful questions in everyday Hebrew:

- כַּמָּה תַּלְמִידִים יֵשׁ בַּכִּתָּה? – kama talmidim yesh ba-kita? – How many students are in the class?
- כַּמָּה מַחְבָּרוֹת יֵשׁ לְךָ? – kama makhbarot yesh lekha? – How many notebooks do you have?
- כַּמָּה זְמַן יֵשׁ לָנוּ? – kama zman yesh lanu? – How much time do we have?

## Numbers in a simple phrase

Useful short patterns to recognize:

- יֵשׁ לִי סֵפֶר אֶחָד – yesh li sefer ekhad – I have one book
- יֵשׁ לִי שְׁתֵּי מַחְבָּרוֹת – yesh li shtei makhbarot – I have two notebooks
- בַּכִּתָּה יֵשׁ שְׁלוֹשָׁה תַּלְמִידִים – ba-kita yesh shlosha talmidim – There are three students in the class
- אֲנַחְנוּ מְחַכִּים חָמֵשׁ דַּקּוֹת – anakhnu mekhakim khamesh dakot – We are waiting five minutes

## What is enough at the beginning

At a beginner level, it is enough to be comfortable with:

- אֶחָד / אַחַת;
- שְׁנֵי / שְׁתֵּי;
- a few common pairs such as שְׁלוֹשָׁה תַּלְמִידִים and שָׁלוֹשׁ דַּקּוֹת;
- the question כַּמָּה;
- three to five short ready-made sentences.

Larger numbers, dates, tens, hundreds, and ordinal numbers belong to later lessons.

## Common mistakes

- Learning “two” only as שתיים. That is the standalone form. Before a noun, you usually need שני / שתי: שני ספרים, שתי מחברות.
- Ignoring the gender of the noun. With numbers, gender is not a decorative detail; it often determines the form you choose.
- Learning numbers without phrases. It is better to remember complete pairs—ספר אחד, שתי מחברות, שלושה תלמידים, חמש דקות—than an abstract list.
- Being discouraged because the system feels illogical. At the beginning, you do not need to solve the whole system; recognize frequent patterns and do not panic over exceptions.
