# Direct objects with אותי, אותך, and אותו

When you need to say not “Dana” or “a book” after a verb, but “him,” “her,” or “me,” Hebrew uses special object forms. This is one of those topics that quickly becomes part of everyday speech: without these forms, a phrase sounds broken or overly unnatural.

For learners, the topic usually becomes easy once the forms are treated not as a case table, but as ready-made blocks after a verb.

Rather than trying to decline pronouns, it is more practical to learn these ready-made forms as common live blocks after a verb.

For example:

- אֲנִי מַכִּיר אוֹתוֹ – ani makir oto – I know him
- אֲנִי מַכִּיר אוֹתָהּ – ani makir ota – I know her

The simplest pattern is:

- subject + action verb + direct-object form

Examples:

- אֲנִי רוֹאֶה אוֹתוֹ – ani ro'e oto – I see him
- הִיא שׁוֹמַעַת אוֹתִי – hi shoma'at oti – she hears me

## Full table of forms

Singular:

- אוֹתִי – oti – me
- אוֹתְךָ – otkha – you (to a man)
- אוֹתָךְ – otakh – you (to a woman)
- אוֹתוֹ – oto – him
- אוֹתָהּ – ota – her

Plural:

- אוֹתָנוּ – otanu – us
- אֶתְכֶם – etkhem – you (men or a mixed group)
- אֶתְכֶן – etkhen – you (a female group)
- אוֹתָם – otam – them (men or a mixed group)
- אוֹתָן – otan – them (a female group)

Note that second-person plural forms are written אֶתְכֶם / אֶתְכֶן (without an extra ו), unlike the other forms, whose base has ו (אוֹת-). This is an isolated spelling exception in the table, but it is read naturally with the usual ת sound.

## The forms are the same in all three tenses

These forms do not change according to the verb’s tense. אוֹתִי, אוֹתוֹ, and אוֹתָהּ work the same way in the present, past, and future:

- אֲנִי רוֹאֶה אוֹתוֹ – I see him (present)
- רָאִיתִי אוֹתוֹ – I saw him (past)
- אֶרְאֶה אוֹתוֹ – I will see him (future)

So it is enough to memorize one set of forms; it then serves all tenses.

## A key trap: אותך in writing

Without niqqud, the form אותך looks the same for a man and a woman, but its pronunciation differs:

- to a man: otkha (אוֹתְךָ)
- to a woman: otakh (אוֹתָךְ)

In real speech, this difference is clearly audible.

## Where this pattern occurs most often

Forms such as אותי, אותו, and אותה occur especially often with these groups of verbs. It is worth learning them in ready-made sentences:

- perception: to see, hear, remember, recognize – לִרְאוֹת, לִשְׁמֹעַ, לִזְכֹּר, לְהַכִּיר;
- attitude: to love, respect, understand – לֶאֱהֹב, לְכַבֵּד, לְהָבִין;
- communication: to invite, ask, call – לְהַזְמִין, לִשְׁאֹל, לִקְרֹא.

## Practical examples

- אֲנִי שׁוֹמֵעַ אוֹתְךָ טוֹב – ani shome'a otkha tov – I can hear you well (to a man)
- אֲנִי שׁוֹמֵעַ אוֹתָךְ טוֹב – ani shome'a otakh tov – I can hear you well (to a woman)
- אֲנַחְנוּ מַזְמִינִים אוֹתָם לָעֶרֶב – anakhnu mazminim otam la-erev – we are inviting them for the evening
- הִיא לֹא מְבִינָה אוֹתָנוּ – hi lo mevina otanu – she does not understand us
- אַתֶּם מַכִּירִים אוֹתָהּ מֵהַלִּמּוּדִים? – atem makirim ota me-ha-limudim? – do you know her from your studies?

## Short pairs: noun → pronoun

- אֲנִי רוֹאֶה אֶת דָּנָה – I see Dana
- אֲנִי רוֹאֶה אוֹתָהּ – I see her

- אֲנַחְנוּ מַכִּירִים אֶת הַמּוֹרֶה – we know the teacher
- אֲנַחְנוּ מַכִּירִים אוֹתוֹ – we know him

- אֲנִי מַזְמִין אֶתְכֶם – I invite you (men or a mixed group)
- אֲנִי מַזְמִינָה אֶתְכֶן – I invite you (a female group)

These pairs clearly show the move from a specific noun to a pronoun and help prevent building a sentence as a word-for-word translation from another language.

## Common mistakes

- A literal pattern such as “I see he.” After a verb like this, use an object form (אוֹתוֹ), not the ordinary pronoun הוּא / הִיא / הֵם.
- Ignoring gender in the second person. In writing, אותך looks the same, but the pronunciation otkha / otakh differs, and that difference is audible in conversation.
- Confusing plural forms. אֶתְכֶם vs אֶתְכֶן and אוֹתָם vs אוֹתָן differ by the type of group. This distinction matters for precise, natural language.
- Omitting the object. When an object can easily be recovered from context, it is sometimes omitted, but in learning speech it is better to name it explicitly.
