# The Jewish Calendar and Holidays

Two calendars operate in parallel in Israel: the Western (Gregorian) calendar and the Jewish lunisolar calendar. The Western calendar is used most often in documents, everyday correspondence, and business. However, all holidays, religious events, dates in the news, and many everyday rhythms of life are tied to the Jewish calendar.

For a learner who lives, or plans to live, in Israel, this is a cultural rather than a grammatical topic. Without it, it is hard to understand why shops close on particular days, how school holidays are arranged, or why the same holiday falls on a different “our-calendar” date each year.

## How the calendar works

A Jewish year has 12 months, each beginning with a new moon. This makes it 10–11 days shorter than a solar year, so a 13th month (**אֲדָר ב׳**) is added from time to time; otherwise, the holidays would gradually shift through the seasons.

The year begins in autumn, with the month of **תִּשְׁרֵי**. Years are counted from the traditional year of the creation of the world; the current years are in the 5700s, the sixth millennium in Jewish reckoning.

## The twelve months

The months in order, beginning in autumn:

- תִּשְׁרֵי – Tishrei (approximately September–October)
- חֶשְׁוָן – Cheshvan (October–November)
- כִּסְלֵו – Kislev (November–December)
- טֵבֵת – Tevet (December–January)
- שְׁבָט – Shvat (January–February)
- אֲדָר – Adar (February–March)
- נִיסָן – Nisan (March–April)
- אִיָּר – Iyar (April–May)
- סִיוָן – Sivan (May–June)
- תַּמּוּז – Tammuz (June–July)
- אָב – Av (July–August)
- אֱלוּל – Elul (August–September)

In a year with an extra month, **אֲדָר ב׳** (Second Adar) follows Adar, and Purim is then observed in that month.

## Autumn: the beginning of the year

The first weeks of **תִּשְׁרֵי** are the most important period in the Jewish calendar. Three major holidays occur then.

- רֹאשׁ הַשָּׁנָה – Rosh HaShanah – New Year (1–2 Tishrei). The beginning of the new year, with traditional foods such as apple with honey (**תַּפּוּחַ בִּדְבַשׁ**), pomegranate, and a fish head.
- יוֹם כִּפּוּר – Yom Kippur – Day of Atonement (10 Tishrei). The holiest day, with a full 25-hour fast. Businesses are closed and public transport does not run.
- סֻכּוֹת – Sukkot – Feast of Tabernacles (15–21 Tishrei). For seven days, temporary booths (**סֻכָּה**) are built and meals are eaten in them.
- שִׂמְחַת תּוֹרָה – Simchat Torah – Rejoicing with the Torah (22 Tishrei). The conclusion of Sukkot; the annual Torah-reading cycle is completed and begun again.

Typical greetings:

- שָׁנָה טוֹבָה – shana tova – have a good year (for Rosh HaShanah)
- גְּמַר חֲתִימָה טוֹבָה – gmar chatima tova – may you be sealed for good (between Rosh HaShanah and Yom Kippur)
- צוֹם קַל – tsom kal – have an easy fast (before Yom Kippur or Tisha B’Av)
- חַג שָׂמֵחַ – chag same'ach – happy holiday (a general greeting)

## Winter

- חֲנֻכָּה – Chanukah – Festival of Lights (25 Kislev, eight days). A **חֲנֻכִּיָּה** is lit each evening, and people eat **סֻפְגָּנִיּוֹת** (jam doughnuts) and **לְבִיבוֹת** (potato pancakes).
- ט״וּ בִּשְׁבָט – Tu BiShvat – New Year of the Trees (15 Shvat). People plant trees and eat dried fruit.

Greeting: **חַג חֲנֻכָּה שָׂמֵחַ** – happy Chanukah.

## Spring

- פּוּרִים – Purim – the Festival of Esther (14 Adar). A carnival-like day with costumes (**תַּחְפּוֹשׂוֹת**), the reading of the **מְגִלָּה** (the Scroll of Esther), and *mishloach manot*, gifts of food (**מִשְׁלוֹחַ מָנוֹת**).
- פֶּסַח – Pesach – Passover (15–22 Nisan). Seven or eight days. The first evening is **סֵדֶר פֶּסַח** (the Passover Seder, with **מַצָּה**). Leavened food (**חָמֵץ**) is not eaten during the festival.
- יוֹם הַשּׁוֹאָה – Yom HaShoah – Holocaust Remembrance Day (27 Nisan). At 10:00, the whole country stops for a two-minute siren and silence.
- יוֹם הַזִּכָּרוֹן – Yom HaZikaron – Memorial Day for fallen soldiers (4 Iyar). There are two minutes of silence.
- יוֹם הָעַצְמָאוּת – Yom HaAtzmaut – Independence Day (5 Iyar). It comes immediately after Yom HaZikaron.
- ל״ג בָּעֹמֶר – Lag BaOmer – the 33rd day of the Omer (18 Iyar). Bonfires and outdoor picnics are common.

Greetings:

- חַג פֶּסַח כָּשֵׁר וְשָׂמֵחַ – chag Pesach kasher ve-same'ach – have a kosher and happy Passover
- פּוּרִים שָׂמֵחַ – Purim same'ach – happy Purim

## Summer

- שָׁבוּעוֹת – Shavuot – Festival of Weeks (6 Sivan). It commemorates the giving of the Torah. Dairy foods (**גְּבִינָה**, **חֲלָבִיוֹת**) are traditionally eaten.
- צוֹם תַּמּוּז – Tzom Tammuz – the Fast of the 17th of Tammuz, beginning the mourning period **בֵּין הַמְּצָרִים**.
- תִּשְׁעָה בְּאָב – Tisha B’Av – the ninth of Av, a day of mourning and fasting that commemorates the destruction of the Temple.

## How to talk about holidays

Here are some typical patterns.

When an event takes place:

- בְּרֹאשׁ הַשָּׁנָה אוֹכְלִים תַּפּוּחַ בִּדְבַשׁ – be-Rosh ha-Shana ochlim tapuach bi-dvash – on Rosh HaShanah, people eat apple with honey
- בְּפֶסַח לֹא אוֹכְלִים חָמֵץ – be-Pesach lo ochlim chamets – on Passover, people do not eat leavened food
- בְּחֲנֻכָּה מַדְלִיקִים נֵרוֹת – be-Chanukah madlikim nerot – on Chanukah, people light candles
- בְּיוֹם כִּפּוּר צָמִים – be-Yom Kippur tsamim – on Yom Kippur, people fast

Asking when a holiday is:

- מָתַי רֹאשׁ הַשָּׁנָה הַשָּׁנָה? – matai Rosh ha-Shana ha-shana? – when is Rosh HaShanah this year?
- בְּאֵיזֶה תַּאֲרִיךְ נוֹפֵל פֶּסַח? – be-eize ta'arikh nofel Pesach? – on what date does Passover fall?

Saying that there will be a day off:

- מָחָר חַג, הֶחָנוּת סְגוּרָה – machar chag, ha-chanut sgura – tomorrow is a holiday; the shop is closed
- בַּיּוֹם הָעַצְמָאוּת אֵין עֲבוֹדָה – ba-Yom ha-Atzmaut ein avoda – there is no work on Independence Day

## Shabbat

Separate from the annual holidays is the weekly **שַׁבָּת** (Shabbat), from sunset on Friday to sunset on Saturday. Most shops, buses, and government offices are closed. Before Shabbat, people say:

- שַׁבָּת שָׁלוֹם – Shabbat shalom – peaceful Shabbat
- שָׁבוּעַ טוֹב – shavua tov – have a good week (at the end of Shabbat, on Saturday evening)

## Ready-to-use short phrases

For everyday use, it is enough to remember a few basic phrases:

- שָׁנָה טוֹבָה – have a good year
- חַג שָׂמֵחַ – happy holiday
- שַׁבָּת שָׁלוֹם – peaceful Shabbat
- צוֹם קַל – have an easy fast
- חֲנֻכָּה שָׂמֵחַ – happy Chanukah
- פּוּרִים שָׂמֵחַ – happy Purim
- חַג פֶּסַח שָׂמֵחַ – happy Passover

## Common mistakes

- Expecting Jewish holidays to fall on the same Western-calendar date each year. The lunisolar calendar shifts relative to the Western one, so **פֶּסַח**, for example, can occur in late March or in the second half of April.
- Confusing **שָׁנָה** (“year”) with **שָׁעָה** (“hour”). They sound similar, but **שָׁנָה טוֹבָה** (“have a good year”) is used only for Rosh HaShanah.
- Using the Western phrase “Happy holidays” in every situation. Each holiday has its own greeting: on Yom Kippur, do not say **חַג שָׂמֵחַ**; say **צוֹם קַל** or **גְּמַר חֲתִימָה טוֹבָה**.
- Thinking that Shabbat is “Sunday.” Shabbat is Saturday and begins on Friday evening. Sunday (**יוֹם רִאשׁוֹן**) is a working day in Israel.
- Ignoring holidays when planning. On major holidays and Shabbat, public transport does not run in most cities and shops are closed. It is worth taking this into account in advance.
