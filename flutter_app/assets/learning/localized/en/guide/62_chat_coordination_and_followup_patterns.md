# Chat: arrangements and follow-ups

Much real language today is made of short messages: “I’m already on my way,” “I’ll be a little late,” “does that work for you?”, “let’s confirm tomorrow,” “let me know when you get there.” These need their own living patterns because chat language is most often short, dense, and free of unnecessary textbook formality.

Chat reveals very quickly whether a phrase sounds natural. Usually, the useful choice is not a complete “correct” line, but a short living message with one clear function.

This topic is different from both a phone call and a formal letter. In a chat, you do not explain everything from scratch or build an official frame: you quickly coordinate, warn, confirm, or remind someone.

In chats, people often:

- arrange a time;
- warn about a change of plan;
- confirm receipt or agreement;
- send a reminder;
- ask someone to signal or write later.

In practice, these patterns are especially common:

- זֶה מַתְאִים לְךָ? – does this work for you?
- אֲנִי בַּדֶּרֶךְ – I am on my way
- אֲנִי מְאַחֵר / מְאַחֶרֶת – I am running late
- סָגַרְנוּ לְמָחָר? – are we set for tomorrow?
- תְּעַדְכֵּן / תְּעַדְכְּנִי אוֹתִי... – let me know...
- תִּכְתֹּב / תִּכְתְּבִי לִי כְּשֶׁתַּגִּיעַ – write to me when you arrive

## Agreeing a time or a plan

One of the most typical short-message formats.

- זֶה מַתְאִים לְךָ? – ze matim lekha? – does this work for you?
- מַתְאִים לְךָ מָחָר בָּעֶרֶב? – matim lekha makhar ba-erev? – does tomorrow evening work for you?
- אֶפְשָׁר בְּשֵׁשׁ בִּמְקוֹם בְּחָמֵשׁ? – efshar be-shesh bimkom be-khamesh? – can we do six instead of five?
- סָגַרְנוּ לְמָחָר? – sagarnu le-makhar? – are we set for tomorrow?
- נִרְאֶה לְךָ הָרַעְיוֹן? – nir'e lekha ha-ra'ayon? – how does the idea sound to you?

## Flexibility with time

Very useful phrases when you need to show that you are not insisting on a particular hour.

- מָתַי שֶׁנּוֹחַ לְךָ – matai she-noakh lekha – whenever is convenient for you
- כָּל זְמַן שֶׁתַּגִּיעַ זֶה בְּסֵדֶר – kol zman she-tagia ze beseder – whenever you arrive is fine
- אֵין לִי בְּעָיָה עִם זֶה – ein li be'aya im ze – I have no problem with that
- תַּגִּיד / תַּגִּידִי לִי שָׁעָה שֶׁמַּתְאִימָה לְךָ – tagid / tagidi li sha'a she-mat'ima lekha – tell me a time that works for you

## Warning about a change

In chat, you often need to warn quickly about a delay or change of plan.

- אֲנִי מְאַחֵר בְּכַמָּה דַּקּוֹת – ani me'akher be-kama dakot – I will be a few minutes late
- אֲנִי בַּדֶּרֶךְ – ani ba-derekh – I am on my way
- אֲנִי לֹא אַסְפִּיק הַיּוֹם – ani lo aspik ha-yom – I will not manage it today
- נִצְטָרֵךְ לִדְחוֹת אֶת זֶה לְמָחָר – nitstarekh lidkhot et ze le-makhar – we will have to move this to tomorrow
- מַשֶּׁהוּ קָרָה, אֶכְתֹּב מָאוֹחָר יוֹתֵר – mashehu kara, ekhtov me'ukhar yoter – something happened; I will write later

## Confirming or giving a short reply

Very short phrases often work here.

- קִבַּלְתִּי – kibalti – received
- מְעֻלֶּה – me'ule – great
- כֵּן, מַתְאִים – ken, matim – yes, that works
- סָגוּר – sagur – agreed; it’s set
- בַּסַּדֶּר גָּמוּר – ba-seder gamur – absolutely fine

## Short live signals

In a friendly or work chat, compact markers such as these are very common. All are conversational:

- סַבַּבָּה – sababa – cool; okay
- יַאלְלָה – yalla – come on; let’s go
- יֵשׁ! – yesh! – great!; yes!
- עַל הַדֶּרֶךְ – al ha-derekh – on the way; soon
- בְּדִיּוּק – be-diyuk – exactly; that’s right

Examples in short replies:

- סַבַּבָּה, אֵשֵׁב בְּשֶׁבַע – okay, I will sit down at seven
- יַאלְלָה, יוֹצְאִים – come on, we are leaving
- בְּדִיּוּק, זֶה מָה שֶׁהִתְכַּוַּנְתִּי – exactly, that is what I meant

## Asking someone to signal you

This is a very frequent conversational pattern.

- תְּעַדְכֵּן אוֹתִי כְּשֶׁאַתָּה מַגִּיעַ – te'aden oti ke-she-ata magia – let me know when you arrive
- תִּכְתְּבִי לִי כְּשֶׁאַתְּ פְּנוּיָה – tikhtevi li ke-she-at pnuya – write to me when you are free
- תּוֹדִיעוּ לִי אִם מַשֶּׁהוּ מִשְׁתַּנֶּה – todi'u li im mashehu mishtane – let me know if anything changes
- תִּשְׁלַח לִי כְּתוֹבֶת כְּשֶׁתּוּכַל – tishlakh li ktovet ke-she-tukhal – send me the address when you can
- תֶּן לִי סִימָן רֶגַע לִפְנֵי – ten li siman rega lifnei – give me a sign a moment beforehand

## Reminding or returning to the conversation

- רַק מַזְכִּיר לְגַבֵּי מָחָר – rak mazkir le-gabei makhar – just a reminder about tomorrow
- לֹא שָׁכַחְתִּי, אֶכְתֹּב לְךָ בָּעֶרֶב – lo shakakhti, ekhtov lekha ba-erev – I have not forgotten; I will write to you this evening
- נַחֲזֹר לָזֶה אַחַר כָּךְ – nakhzor la-ze akhar kakh – we will return to this later
- דַּבֵּר אִתִּי כְּשֶׁנּוֹחַ לְךָ – daber iti ke-she-noakh lekha – get in touch when it is convenient for you

## How this sounds in live speech

In chat, short, living phrases win:

- אֲנִי בַּדֶּרֶךְ
- אֲנִי מְאַחֵר קְצָת
- מַתְאִים לְךָ?
- תְּעַדְכֵּן אוֹתִי
- סָגוּר
- סַבַּבָּה, מָתַי שֶׁנּוֹחַ לְךָ

This sounds more real than overly complete or official sentences in every message.

## Common mistakes

- Writing too formally in chat. In neutral brief correspondence, that often sounds heavy and unnatural.
- Being too fragmentary without context. Brevity helps, but sometimes you need to add one grounding detail: which time, which meeting, or what exactly may change.
- Not warning about a change of plan. אֲנִי מְאַחֵר, אֲנִי בַּדֶּרֶךְ, and לֹא אַסְפִּיק are very important for live communication.
- Confusing a friendly chat with formal correspondence. The same patterns may be excellent in chat but too conversational for an official request.
- Overusing conversational markers such as סבבה and יאללה. In a work chat with someone you do not know well, neutral בְּסֵדֶר, בְּדִיּוּק, and מַתְאִים are better.
