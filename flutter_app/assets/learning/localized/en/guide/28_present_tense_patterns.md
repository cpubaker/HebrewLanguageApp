# Present tense: forms and usage

The Hebrew present tense is best learned not as an abstract table, but as a system of forms that immediately show who is performing an action. The key is not to derive a rule from scratch every time, but to learn to recognize gender and number quickly in the word itself.

In practice, this is one of the most convenient verb topics: once your eye gets used to a few typical endings, forms begin to read quite naturally.

The Hebrew present tense works somewhat differently from English:

- a form often resembles an adjective;
- person is not marked in the form itself; only the pronoun shows it;
- gender and number are visible directly in the word.

Most common verbs have four basic forms:

- masculine singular;
- feminine singular;
- masculine plural;
- feminine plural.

Typical ending clues:

- feminine singular often ends in ת;
- masculine plural often ends in ־ים;
- feminine plural often ends in ־ות.

## The basic פָּעַל (קַל) pattern

This is the most common pattern. The feminine form usually gains a ת ending, while the plural adds ־ים / ־ות.

### לִלְמֹד – to study

- לוֹמֵד – lomed – studies; is studying (masculine singular)
- לוֹמֶדֶת – lomedet – studies; is studying (feminine singular)
- לוֹמְדִים – lomdim – study; are studying (masculine plural)
- לוֹמְדוֹת – lomdot – study; are studying (feminine plural)

### לִכְתֹּב – to write

- כּוֹתֵב – kotev – writes; is writing (masculine singular)
- כּוֹתֶבֶת – kotevet – writes; is writing (feminine singular)
- כּוֹתְבִים – kotvim – write; are writing (masculine plural)
- כּוֹתְבוֹת – kotvot – write; are writing (feminine plural)

### לַעֲבֹד – to work

- עוֹבֵד – oved – works; is working (masculine singular)
- עוֹבֶדֶת – ovedet – works; is working (feminine singular)
- עוֹבְדִים – ovdim – work; are working (masculine plural)
- עוֹבְדוֹת – ovdot – work; are working (feminine plural)

## Verbs with a final ה present-tense pattern

In a group of verbs such as לִשְׁתּוֹת, לִרְצוֹת, and לִקְנוֹת, the masculine and feminine singular present forms end in ה rather than following the more common feminine ת pattern:

### לִשְׁתּוֹת – to drink

- שׁוֹתֶה – shote
- שׁוֹתָה – shota
- שׁוֹתִים – shotim
- שׁוֹתוֹת – shotot

### לִרְצוֹת – to want

- רוֹצֶה – rotse
- רוֹצָה – rotsa
- רוֹצִים – rotsim
- רוֹצוֹת – rotsot

### לִקְנוֹת – to buy

- קוֹנֶה – kone
- קוֹנָה – kona
- קוֹנִים – konim
- קוֹנוֹת – konot

Here, the masculine form ends in ֶה (e) and the feminine form in ָה (a), without an additional ת.

## Patterns from other binyanim

In פִּעֵל, הִפְעִיל, and הִתְפַּעֵל, present-tense forms begin with מ. This is one of the most visible clues.

### פִּעֵל – active verbs beginning with מ

- מְדַבֵּר / מְדַבֶּרֶת – medaber / medaberet – speaks (לְדַבֵּר)
- מְבַקֵּשׁ / מְבַקֶּשֶׁת – mevakesh / mevakeshet – asks; requests (לְבַקֵּשׁ)
- מְסַפֵּר / מְסַפֶּרֶת – mesaper / mesaperet – tells (לְסַפֵּר)

### הִפְעִיל – often a causative action

- מַסְבִּיר / מַסְבִּירָה – masbir / masbira – explains (לְהַסְבִּיר)
- מַתְחִיל / מַתְחִילָה – matkhil / matkhila – begins (לְהַתְחִיל)
- מַכְנִיס / מַכְנִיסָה – makhnis / makhnisa – brings in; puts inside (לְהַכְנִיס)

### הִתְפַּעֵל – often directed at oneself, beginning with מִת

- מִתְלַבֵּשׁ / מִתְלַבֶּשֶׁת – mitlabesh / mitlabeshet – gets dressed (לְהִתְלַבֵּשׁ)
- מִתְרַחֵץ / מִתְרַחֶצֶת – mitrakhets / mitrakhetset – washes oneself (לְהִתְרַחֵץ)
- מִתְעוֹרֵר / מִתְעוֹרֶרֶת – mit'orer / mit'oreret – wakes up (לְהִתְעוֹרֵר)

The prefix מ / מִת is a very useful binyan clue, especially when the infinitive is not nearby in the sentence.

## Verb or adjective?

Some present-tense forms look like adjectives.

Compare:

- הוּא עוֹבֵד – hu oved – he works (an action)
- הוּא עָיֵף – hu ayef – he is tired (a state)

The words can look similar, but they play different roles in the sentence:

- עובד answers “what is he doing?”;
- עייף answers “what is he like?” or “what state is he in?”

If a word shows an action, it is a verb. If it describes a state or quality, it is an adjective.

## Word order and focus

Neutral word order is very simple:

- subject + present-tense form + object or adverbial phrase

Examples:

- אֲנִי קוֹרֵא סֵפֶר בַּבַּיִת – ani kore sefer ba-bayit – I read a book at home
- הִיא כּוֹתֶבֶת הוֹדָעָה עַכְשָׁו – hi kotevet hoda'a akhshav – she is writing a message now
- אֲנַחְנוּ עוֹבְדִים בָּעֶרֶב – anakhnu ovdim ba-erev – we work in the evening

## Short pairs

- אֲנִי לוֹמֵד עִבְרִית – I study Hebrew (said by a man)
- אֲנִי לוֹמֶדֶת עִבְרִית – I study Hebrew (said by a woman)

- הֵם עוֹבְדִים כָּאן – they work here (men or a mixed group)
- הֵן עוֹבְדוֹת כָּאן – they work here (a female group)

- אַתָּה כּוֹתֵב מַהֵר – you write quickly (to a man)
- אַתְּ כּוֹתֶבֶת מַהֵר – you write quickly (to a woman)

## Common mistakes

- Using the masculine form as universal. This is a common beginner habit, but it prevents precise, natural speech.
- Confusing a verb with an adjective. The problem is usually not vocabulary, but failing to check whether the word is an action or a description of a state.
- Mechanically transferring plural endings. Not every form follows one pattern. It is safer to memorize several common verbs completely than to guess every new form.
- Ignoring feminine plural. For a group made up only of women, the form ending in ות is normal and important for meaning.
- Not noticing the מ / מִת prefix. In פיעל, הפעיל, and התפעל forms, the beginning of the word often reveals the binyan and helps prevent confusion with a similar adjective.
