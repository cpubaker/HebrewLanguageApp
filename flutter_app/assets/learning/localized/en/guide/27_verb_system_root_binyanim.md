# The verb root and binyan

When forms such as לִכְתֹּב, כּוֹתֵב, כָּתַב, and יִכְתֹּב appear together, it is easy to treat them as four separate words. In fact, they belong to the same verb family. The main goal of this topic is to learn to recognize a familiar verb even when its form has changed.

This is one of the topics that sounds more difficult at first than it proves to be in practice. Once you begin to see the common base in different forms, a verb stops breaking apart into a collection of separate words.

In its simplest form, a Hebrew verb can be read like this:

- the root, שורש, gives the word’s core idea;
- the binyan, בניין, shows which familiar verb pattern this idea is “wearing”;
- the specific form shows the tense and sometimes also person, gender, and number.

In other words, it is best to see a verb not as one form, but as a whole connected system.

## The root

A root most often consists of three consonants. It usually carries the word’s central meaning.

The root כ־ת־ב gives a family of words about writing:

- לִכְתֹּב – likhtov – to write
- כּוֹתֵב – kotev – writes; is writing
- כָּתַב – katav – wrote
- יִכְתֹּב – yikhtov – will write

The root ל־מ־ד gives a family about learning:

- לִלְמֹד – lilmod – to study; to learn
- לוֹמֵד – lomed – studies; is studying
- לָמַד – lamad – studied
- יִלְמַד – yilmed – will study

The root is not always equally visible in every form, but it often helps you understand that you are not seeing a new word, but a familiar family.

## What is a binyan?

It is best to think of a binyan not as an intimidating term, but as a type of verb pattern. The same root can appear in different familiar patterns, which shifts the meaning slightly.

At the beginning, there is no need to learn all the binyan names. It is more important to understand the principle: a verb has a shared base and a pattern that suggests how the word works.

## One root, different roles

This is clear with the root ל־מ־ד:

- לִלְמֹד – lilmod – to study; to learn
- לוֹמֵד – lomed – studies; is studying
- לְלַמֵּד – lelamed – to teach (someone)
- מְלַמֵּד – melamed – teaches

The idea of learning remains shared, but the role of the verb is different: “to study” and “to teach” are not the same thing.

## The seven binyanim: a general map

Hebrew has only seven binyanim. At the beginning, it is enough to know their names and their general roles. Active forms are more common:

Active (4):

- פָּעַל (קַל) – a basic, neutral action
- פִּעֵל – often an active, more intensive, or directed action
- הִפְעִיל – often a causative action: causing something or putting something into action
- הִתְפַּעֵל – often an action directed at oneself or a process of change

Passive / reflexive (3):

- נִפְעַל – the passive counterpart of פעל
- פֻּעַל – the passive counterpart of פיעל
- הֻפְעַל – the passive counterpart of הפעיל

Passive forms are covered in a separate lesson; at the start, it is enough to recognize the four active patterns.

## The most common binyanim: examples

### פָּעַל / קַל – a basic action

- לִכְתֹּב – to write
- לִלְמֹד – to study; to learn
- לִקְרוֹא – to read
- לָלֶכֶת – to go

### פִּעֵל – often active or intensive

- לְלַמֵּד – to teach
- לְדַבֵּר – to speak
- לְבַקֵּשׁ – to ask; to request
- לְסַפֵּר – to tell

### הִפְעִיל – causative, “to make something happen”

- לְהַכְנִיס – to bring in; to put inside
- לְהַסְבִּיר – to explain
- לְהַתְחִיל – to begin
- לְהַרְאוֹת – to show

### הִתְפַּעֵל – directed at oneself or a process

- לְהִתְלַבֵּשׁ – to get dressed
- לְהִתְרַחֵץ – to wash oneself
- לְהִתְעוֹרֵר – to wake up
- לְהִתְקָרֵב – to get closer

These are general clues rather than an exact formula. A binyan helps you see a word family, but common individual verbs are still best learned through real examples.

## How to read a verb in a reference

When you meet a new verb, it is useful to look at five things right away:

- the infinitive;
- one natural present-tense form;
- one past-tense form;
- one future-tense form;
- whether it follows a familiar pattern or is best memorized immediately as a frequent exception.

Example notes for לִכְתֹּב:

- לִכְתֹּב – to write
- כּוֹתֵב / כּוֹתֶבֶת – writes; is writing
- כָּתַב – wrote
- יִכְתֹּב – will write

This kind of entry is much more useful than the translation “to write” alone.

## What you can see in a form

Even without a large table, simple things are often visible:

- the infinitive often begins with ל;
- the future tense often has an א / ת / י / נ prefix at the beginning;
- the present tense often behaves more like an adjective and changes for gender and number;
- the past tense is often recognizable through personal endings.

So it is better to look at a verb alongside several of its forms, not by itself.

## When the rule does not work perfectly

Hebrew likes systems, but its most common verbs often have forms that are awkward to calculate from a general rule alone. That is normal: a living language nearly always preserves a few very frequent forms separately.

For example:

- הָיָה – to be
- הָלַךְ – to go
- בָּא – to come
- נָתַן – to give

It is best to treat such words both as part of the system and as a set of ready-made frequent forms.

## What to learn first

To avoid overload, it is useful to proceed in this order:

1. the infinitive and its basic meaning;
2. a present-tense form;
3. a past-tense “I” or “he” form;
4. a future-tense “I” or “he” form;
5. only then, a broader table and the binyan name.

This order does not get in the way of understanding the system, but makes it useful for real reading.

## Common mistakes

- Treating every form as a separate word. When כּוֹתֵב, כָּתַב, and יִכְתֹּב are learned separately, you create extra work. It is more useful to see them as one family.
- Expecting a binyan always to give an exact translation. A binyan often suggests a direction, but does not replace the word’s real meaning. Not everything can be guessed from the pattern alone.
- Learning only the infinitive. The infinitive is useful by itself, but it is not enough for living language. You need to see at least one present, past, and future form.
- Being intimidated by the terms שורש and בניין. At the start, there is no need to learn all grammatical terminology. It is enough to understand the idea: a verb has a shared base and a pattern in which that base appears.
